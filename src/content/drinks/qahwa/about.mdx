Coffee moved through Yemeni ports as one of the region's major traded goods
long before it settled into the ceremony now associated with it, and gahwa
grew from what began as a simple gesture of welcome toward travellers into a
formal ritual of diplomacy and daily hospitality across the Gulf
<Cite ref="national"/>. Cardamom is the constant in the recipe, with cloves,
saffron and occasionally rosewater used alongside it depending on the
household <Cite ref="gulfnews"/> — a lighter roast and a shorter simmer than
Turkish coffee, poured pale and without sugar.

The vessel is as much a part of the tradition as the brew. The dallah, the
long-spouted pot with a domed lid used to simmer and serve it, takes its name
from an Arabic root meaning to pamper, and regional forms vary by
craftsmanship, with the Iraqi al-Baghdadiya dallah considered among the
oldest and finest still made <Cite ref="gulfnews"/>. In the United Arab
Emirates the pot's standing was formalised outright: it has appeared on the
national one-dirham coin since 1973 <Cite ref="national"/>.

Declining a cup when it is offered carries real weight. The pour itself is
small and repeated rather than a single full serving, and refusing it, or
failing to offer it to a guest, is read as a breach of hospitality rather than
a simple preference <Cite ref="national"/>. That social role, alongside the
brewing method itself, is what UNESCO recognised in 2015 when Arabic coffee
culture was added to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural
Heritage of Humanity <Cite ref="gulfnews"/>.
