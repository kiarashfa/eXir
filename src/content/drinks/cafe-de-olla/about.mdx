The drink's association with the Mexican Revolution of 1910 to 1920 is
specific rather than general: it is credited to the soldaderas, the women
who travelled with revolutionary troops as cooks, medics and combatants and
are remembered locally as adelitas <Cite ref="ojodelago"/>. Coffee in a
military camp was routinely reheated and overworked until it turned harshly
bitter, and the adelitas' response was to sweeten and spice it rather than
brew it better with what they had — piloncillo and cinnamon went in to mask
what repeated reheating had done, and the result outlasted the war it was
improvised for <Cite ref="ojodelago"/>.

The clay pot is not incidental to that story; it is where the drink gets its
name. *Olla de barro* is unglazed earthenware, and both the method and most
sources treat the pot as essential rather than traditional-but-optional: clay
distributes heat more gently than metal and is credited with giving the
coffee an earthy character a saucepan does not reproduce <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>
<Cite ref="ojodelago"/>. The base recipe is coffee, cinnamon and piloncillo,
with orange peel, aniseed or cloves added in regional and family versions
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — a spread of variation consistent with a drink that
spread by household rather than by recipe card.

It remains most associated with cold weather and rural households rather
than city cafes <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>, closer to a drink passed down within
families than to a commercially standardised one, which is also why versions
of it disagree on exactly which spices belong in the pot.
