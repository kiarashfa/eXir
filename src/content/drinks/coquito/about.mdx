Coquito's name is straightforward — "little coconut" in Spanish
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — but its origin is not documented with anything
like the same precision. Both sources agree on the broad shape of the story:
Spanish colonists in Puerto Rico brought their own milk-and-spirit punch,
in the general lineage of British and American eggnog, and combined it with
coconut milk and locally distilled rum to produce something distinct from
either <Cite ref="wikipedia"/> <Cite ref="hispanicfoodnetwork"/>. Neither
source names an inventor or a date, and one states plainly that who created
it and how the recipe began are simply not known
<Cite ref="hispanicfoodnetwork"/>.

The rum most associated with older versions of the drink is pitorro, an
unaged, high-proof Puerto Rican cane spirit distilled well above standard
commercial strength, though modern recipes commonly substitute a standard
dark or spiced rum instead <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What has more clearly
shifted over time is the dairy: today's standard build layers coconut milk,
cream of coconut, sweetened condensed milk and evaporated milk together,
the latter two being manufactured products that entered Puerto Rican
kitchens once American food companies had a commercial presence on the
island, rather than ingredients available to whoever first combined coconut
with a milk punch <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Egg is the one ingredient that splits current practice rather than
history. Stovetop versions of coquito, cooked briefly before the rum and
coconut are added, more often include egg yolk for body; recipes built
entirely in a blender frequently leave it out <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Both
are called coquito, and the difference is a matter of household preference
rather than a documented regional split.
