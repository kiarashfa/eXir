Sugarcane was already being grown for its juice across South and Southeast
Asia for centuries before it was processed into crystallised sugar, which is
the older use of the plant <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The food historian Richard
Foss traces India's documented relationship with cane back further still:
citing the Indian historian Ziauddin Barani, Foss notes that a 1316 decree
by a Delhi sultan banning the distillation of sugarcane spirit is itself
evidence the practice — and by extension cane processing more broadly —
was established well before that date, since a ruler generally only outlaws
something already in use <Cite ref="foss"/>.

Fresh-pressed juice rather than the crystallised product remains the more
common street drink across much of the region today, sold from cane
presses in India, Pakistan and neighbouring countries, typically finished
with lime and sometimes ginger <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The practice is not
confined to South Asia: an inscription from 9th-century Java records a sweet
drink called Nalaka Rasa, "sugarcane juice," suggesting the same fresh-pressed
tradition took hold independently across the cane-growing regions of
Southeast Asia at a comparably early date <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Regional
versions today vary in what is added at the press — lime and ginger in
India, lime or pineapple in Brazil, where the drink is called caldo de cana
or garapa, and salted lime in Vietnam <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Pakistan declared
sugarcane juice its national drink in 2019 <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
