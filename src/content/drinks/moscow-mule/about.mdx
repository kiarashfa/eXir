The accounts agree on the people and disagree on almost everything else. A
1948 newspaper story credited three men — Jack Morgan, owner of Cock 'n
Bull ginger beer, John G. Martin, who held the American rights to Smirnoff,
and Rudolph Kunett, head of Smirnoff's vodka division — with combining the
two products at Manhattan's Chatham Hotel in 1941 <Cite ref="wiki"/>. A
later account, drawn from an interview with Cock 'n Bull bartender Wes
Price, places the invention a year earlier at the restaurant's Los Angeles
bar, with Price himself mixing the drink to use up unsold vodka and ginger
beer <Cite ref="wehohistory"/>. Both versions keep the same underlying
problem: neither Smirnoff nor Cock 'n Bull ginger beer was selling, and
someone poured them together to move the stock.

The copper mug is where the record thins out further. Martin's own account
names Oseline Schmidt, said to have inherited a copper works, as its
source; other tellings credit Sophie Berezinski, a Russian immigrant whose
father ran the Moscow Copper Company. Neither claim is independently
verified <Cite ref="wehohistory"/>.

What is not disputed is how the drink was sold. Martin spent years
travelling to bars across the United States, asking bartenders to pose with
a mug and a Smirnoff bottle so he could photograph them with an instant
camera — proof, carried to the next bar, that the drink was already
catching on elsewhere <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The tactic worked well enough to
help move American vodka from a marginal curiosity to the country's
best-selling spirit within a generation <Cite ref="wehohistory"/>.
