Fermented cereal drinks of this general kind are documented in Anatolia and
Mesopotamia many thousands of years before Boza is recorded as a name, and
the word itself is attested among Central Asian Turkic peoples by the tenth
century, spreading from there into the Caucasus and the Balkans as Turkic
and later Ottoman rule expanded <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The seventeenth-century
Ottoman traveller Evliya Çelebi recorded that it was popular with the
Janissaries, who valued it as a low-alcohol drink they could carry on
campaign, and that Istanbul alone held some three hundred boza shops
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

What Evliya's account makes clear is that "boza" was never one drink. He
distinguished a sweet, mild, largely non-alcoholic style he considered fit
for pious and learned people from a sour, longer-fermented "Tatar" boza sold
in port taverns to sailors and labourers, a distinction the food historian
Mary Işın has also drawn <Cite ref="cornucopia"/>. Ottoman authorities
periodically restricted or banned the drink over the centuries, largely
because of that stronger, more intoxicating version rather than the sweet
one <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. By the nineteenth century the mild, low-alcohol
style had become the dominant one at the Ottoman court, and it is the style
that survived into ordinary retail sale <Cite ref="cornucopia"/>.

Istanbul's Vefa Bozacısı, founded in 1876 and still trading from the same
shop today, is the best-documented survivor of that retail trade
<Cite ref="cornucopia"/>. Its thick, tart style, served cold and traditionally
topped with cinnamon and roasted chickpeas, is close to what a modern reader
now expects the drink to mean, even though it was originally just one of
several regional styles sold under the same name.
