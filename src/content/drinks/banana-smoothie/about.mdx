Blending fruit into a thick, drinkable purée needed a machine that did not
exist commercially before the 1930s. Fred Waring and Fred Osius spent that
decade developing a blender powerful enough to purée whole fruit, released as
the Waring Blendor <Cite ref="daily"/>. Health food stores on the US west
coast took it up soon after, and by the 1960s smoothies were an established
part of a wider health food movement built around vegetarianism, whole foods
and a turn away from processed ones <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="daily"/>.

The drink stayed a health food store item rather than a mainstream one until
Steve Kuhnau opened the first Smoothie King in 1973, after several years of
blending his own fruit and juice combinations as a lactose-intolerant teenager
looking for something closer to a milkshake he could actually drink
<Cite ref="daily"/>. Smoothie King's franchising turned a homemade fix into a
category, and other chains followed, eventually pulling the smoothie away from
its health food origins with sweeter, less austere recipes aimed at a wider
market <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The banana smoothie itself was never a single invention. It sits at the
plainest end of what the blender and the health food movement made possible:
fruit, a liquid and no more engineering than the two required, which is
likely why it carries no origin story of its own the way the branded and
flavoured versions that followed it do.
