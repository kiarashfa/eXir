Paper-filtered coffee begins with a specific registration. Melitta Bentz filed
her design with the Imperial Patent Office in Berlin on 20 June 1908, and it was
granted on 8 July as a utility model described as a coffee filter with a domed
underside, a recessed bottom and inclined flow holes <Cite ref="dpma"/>. The
problem it solved was grounds in the cup and bitterness from over-extraction, and
the solution was paper — which removes both by removing the grounds from the
liquid the moment brewing stops.

The method was redesigned as a scientific instrument rather than a kitchen
convenience by Peter Schlumbohm, whose Chemex dates from 1941
<Cite ref="chemex"/>. Its filter is markedly thicker than a drip filter, which
holds back most of the oils and produces a cup usually described as clean
<Cite ref="chemex"/> — a deliberate trade of body for clarity, and the first
time the paper itself was treated as a variable worth choosing.

The current specialty form is more recent still. Hario's V60 dates from 2004 and
is named for its shape: a cone at a sixty-degree angle, with spiral ribs added so
that water runs down the sides rather than being held against them by the paper
<Cite ref="hario"/>. Between the three of them the equipment has changed far less
than the intent has. Bentz was removing a nuisance; the modern cone exists to
make every variable adjustable.

Two things distinguish this from every other drink documented here. The first is
that it scales by ratio rather than by count: doubling the cup means doubling the
dose and the water together, while the temperature, the grind and the contact
time stay exactly where they are. The second is that most of what is weighed out
never arrives. About a fifth of the dose dissolves; the rest stays in the filter,
along with a tenth of the water, which is why the cup is smaller than the kettle
suggests and why the figures here are stated against what reaches it.
