"Margarita" is Spanish for daisy, and the drink's shape — a spirit, citrus and
a sweetened liqueur, shaken — is the tequila branch of the older Daisy family
of cocktails <Cite ref="diffords"/>. A Tequila Daisy served in Tijuana is
recorded in the *Moville Mail* on 23 July 1936, credited to a bartender named
Madden who was said to have grabbed the wrong bottle by accident
<Cite ref="diffords"/>.

No single origin for the Margarita specifically is settled, and the claims
that survive contradict each other on both the year and the inventor. Johnny
Durlesser, a bartender at the Tail O' The Cock in Los Angeles, said he
created it in 1937, though his own recipe matches one already published that
year in the *Café Royal Cocktail Book* under a different name
<Cite ref="tequila512"/>. Danny Herrera is said to have made it at Rancho La
Gloria for the actress Marjorie King, who could tolerate tequila but not
other spirits, in an account usually dated to 1938 <Cite ref="diffords"/>
<Cite ref="tequila512"/>. Francisco "Pancho" Morales claimed to have
improvised it at Tommy's Place in Ciudad Juárez in 1942 after mishearing an
order <Cite ref="diffords"/> <Cite ref="tequila512"/>. Margaret Sames, a
Texan socialite, is separately credited with serving it at a Christmas party
in Acapulco in 1948, an account that reached a wider audience once her guest
Conrad Hilton put the drink on his hotels' menus <Cite ref="diffords"/>.

None of these accounts is independently verifiable, and several were only
put on record decades after the events they describe. What the competing
stories agree on is a period — the late 1930s through the late 1940s, on
both sides of the US–Mexico border — rather than a single bar or a single
inventor.
