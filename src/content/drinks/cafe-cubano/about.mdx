Coffee reached Cuba well before the drink named for it did. French colonists
fleeing the Haitian Revolution brought cultivation to the island in the
eighteenth century, and by the mid-1800s Cuba had grown into a major
producer, reaching over sixty thousand tons of coffee a year by the 1940s
<Cite ref="volcanica"/>. The espresso machine that defines café Cubano today
arrived separately and much later, carried by Italian immigration in the
early twentieth century, and it was Cuban baristas who adapted the Italian
machine to a preparation of their own rather than copying an existing recipe
<Cite ref="baristamag"/>.

That adaptation is the espuma, and it is the drink's whole identity. The
first few drops off the machine, the most concentrated part of the shot, are
poured onto sugar and beaten hard enough to turn pale and foamy before the
rest of the coffee is added; poured back in, the foam rises to the surface
rather than dissolving into the cup <Cite ref="baristamag"/>. Demerara sugar
is the traditional choice, and the whipping technique is what separates a
café Cubano from an ordinary sweetened espresso — the sugar is aerated into
the coffee, not merely dissolved in it.

The Cuban Revolution of the 1950s carried the drink out of Cuba rather than
changing it: the wave of Cuban migration to Miami and other American cities
that followed brought café Cubano culture with it, and Miami's Cuban coffee
windows are now as associated with the drink as Havana is <Cite ref="volcanica"/>.
The same espuma technique, scaled up, makes a colada meant for sharing; with
milk added it becomes a cortadito, and with more milk still, a café con leche.
