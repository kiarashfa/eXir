The pot was invented by Alfonso Bialetti at his metalworking shop in
Crusinallo, in Piedmont, in 1933 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Bialetti had spent
years working in the French aluminium industry before returning home, and the
Moka Express applied that material to a shape said to be inspired by watching
his wife's laundry machine push soapy water up through a central tube and back
down over the clothes <Cite ref="essential-italy"/>. The pot does the same
thing with water and steam: sealed in a lower chamber over heat, the water is
forced up through a funnel of grounds and collects in the upper chamber, no
pump or plunger involved.

It was not an immediate success. Only around seventy thousand pots sold in its
first six years <Cite ref="essential-italy"/>. Growth came after the Second
World War, when Alfonso's son Renato took over the company and marketed it
under the line "in casa un espresso come al bar" — an espresso at home, just
like the one at the bar <Cite ref="essential-italy"/>, though the pot's one to
two bar of pressure is well short of what a café machine reaches and the
result is closer to a very strong filter coffee than to true espresso. By 1956
the factory was producing eighteen thousand pots a day <Cite ref="essential-italy"/>,
and the design — octagonal, aluminium, unchanged in its basic engineering —
is now reported to sit in the large majority of Italian households
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The method rewards a particular kind of attention: moderate heat rather than
high, because the pressure that drives the water is building on its own, and a
readiness to take the pot off the stove the moment the flow turns pale and
starts to sputter, before the empty chamber has a chance to scorch what little
coffee is left in the funnel.
