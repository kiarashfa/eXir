The word predates the drink by centuries. The Capuchin order of Franciscan
friars, formed in 1529, wore a plain, hooded robe in a light reddish-brown
<Cite ref="altalang"/>, and the Italian name for that hood — *cappuccio* —
gave the order its own name, Cappuccini <Cite ref="aleteia"/>. Neither source
connects the friars to coffee directly; the link runs through the colour of
the robe, applied later to a drink that happened to come out roughly the same
shade.

That application first shows up not in Italy but in eighteenth-century
Vienna, where coffee topped with whipped cream was served under the German
name *kapuziner*, again for its colour <Cite ref="altalang"/>. The drink
familiar today is a twentieth-century Italian reworking of that idea: once
espresso machines were common, whipped cream was replaced with steamed,
foamed milk, but the Italian form of the old name — cappuccino — was kept
<Cite ref="altalang"/>. What survived from Vienna to Italy was the colour
comparison and the name it produced, not the recipe.

The modern proportions — roughly equal parts espresso, steamed milk and
foam — are an Italian espresso-bar convention rather than anything the
friars or the Viennese kapuziner determined, and the deep, stiff foam a
cappuccino is served under is what most reliably tells it apart from a
caffè latte's thinner cap over a taller glass of milk.
