Kvass appears in the *Primary Chronicle*, the earliest surviving history of
the Kievan state, in an account of Prince Vladimir's baptism in 988 or 989,
where kvass is distributed to the people of Kiev alongside honey and food
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/><Cite ref="atlasobscura"/>. It is drunk almost purely as
a household ferment for most of its history: rye bread or rye flour and malt
steeped in hot water, then fermented for about half a day with bread yeast
and sugar, a process simple and forgiving enough that nearly any household
could keep it going <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Its low alcohol content and the
fermentation's antibacterial effect also made it a safer everyday drink than
untreated water in a medieval city, which is part of why monastic
communities favoured it <Cite ref="atlasobscura"/>.

The drink stayed largely a home and tavern product until the nineteenth
century, when commercial and eventually industrial production turned it
into an ordinary consumer good rather than something made in every kitchen
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The Soviet state later gave it a second, openly
political life: in 1964, Premier Nikita Khrushchev announced plans to sell
kvass internationally as a native alternative to Western sodas, and the
following decades saw it sold from tanker trucks and distinctive yellow
street barrels across Soviet cities <Cite ref="atlasobscura"/>. Sales fell
after the Soviet Union's collapse as Western brands entered the market, then
recovered sharply in the 2000s as producers marketed kvass explicitly as a
patriotic, natural alternative to imported cola — the same contrast the
Soviet campaign had drawn decades earlier, now aimed at a different audience
<Cite ref="atlasobscura"/>.
