No bar or bartender is reliably credited with the Paloma, and the drink
could not have existed before Squirt grapefruit soda went into production
in Arizona in 1938 <Cite ref="tastingtable"/>. Squirt was not sold in Mexico
until 1955, though tequila was already being mixed with carbonated sodas
there under the name changuirongo before that, so which side of the border
the specific tequila-and-grapefruit combination first appeared on is not
settled <Cite ref="tastingtable"/>.

Don Javier Delgado Corona, the longtime owner of La Capilla in the town of
Tequila, Jalisco, is sometimes named as the drink's creator, but the claim
rests mostly on his authorship of a different grapefruit-soda cocktail, the
Batanga, and he never claimed the Paloma himself <Cite ref="tastingtable"/>
<Cite ref="chilled"/>. In the United States, the drink's spread is tied to
Texas rather than to Mexico directly: a pamphlet by bartender Evan Harrison,
titled after the citrus-growing Rio Grande Valley, helped popularise it
alongside the region's own Ruby Red grapefruit, itself the first patented
citrus variety in the country <Cite ref="chilled"/>.

The name — Spanish for "dove" — has no documented source. It is treated by
some as a nod to the drink's pale colour and by others as a reference to an
older Mexican folk song of the same title, but neither claim is verified.
The version most often served in Mexico today still leans on bottled
grapefruit soda, usually Jarritos, rather than fresh juice and a separate
sparkling top; the fresh-juice version more common outside Mexico is a
later, bar-driven variation on the same simple build <Cite ref="chilled"/>.
