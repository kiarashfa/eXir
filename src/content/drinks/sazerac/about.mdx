The name comes from a brand of cognac, Sazerac-de-Forge et Fils, imported into
New Orleans around 1850 by the merchant Sewell T. Taylor <Cite ref="diffords"/>.
Aaron Bird, who ran the Merchants Exchange Coffee House on Exchange Alley,
began serving a cocktail built on that cognac, and the bitters said to have
gone into it from the start were made by the local apothecary Antoine Amédée
Peychaud, who is credited with formulating them in 1834 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The drink's own history is not settled beyond that point. The most-repeated
account traces its assembly to Bird's bar rather than to Peychaud personally,
and the historian David Wondrich has argued the popular version of events
compresses a longer, messier commercial history — including the possibility
that an early form was sold bottled by the Sazerac company itself rather than
mixed to order <Cite ref="diffords"/>. Both readings agree on the two changes
that turned the drink into the one served today. Around 1870, when Thomas H.
Handy took over what by then was called the Sazerac Coffee House, cognac gave
way to American rye whiskey after phylloxera devastated the French vineyards
that supplied it <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. An absinthe rinse was added to the
glass at some point in the same period, and when absinthe was banned in the
United States in 1912 the Sazerac company introduced its own substitute,
Herbsaint, which stood in for it for decades afterward <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

On June 23, 2008, the Louisiana Legislature named the Sazerac the state's
official cocktail of the city of New Orleans <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>, a
designation with a firm date even where the drink's earlier decades are
disputed.

The rinse rather than a poured measure of absinthe is the detail that
survives every retelling. It is what lets a spirit strong enough to flavour
the whole glass appear only as a scent on the nose, and it is also the reason
the drink is stirred separately from the glass it is served in rather than
built inside it the way its plainer, older relatives are.
