The build itself is not Japanese in origin. Brandy and soda was a
fashionable English drink from the early nineteenth century, and when the
Napoleonic wars cut off cognac supply, Scotch took its place; by 1900, by
the cocktail historian David Wondrich's account, the Scotch highball was
the most fashionable drink in the United States <Cite ref="scmp"/>.

What Japan did was give the same two ingredients a national home. Shinjiro
Torii founded Suntory's Yamazaki distillery in 1923, the country's first
commercial whisky distillery, and after the Second World War the company
opened Torys Bars serving whisky highballs to office workers across major
cities — eventually more than two thousand branches nationwide
<Cite ref="broadsheet"/>. That made the highball an inexpensive, everyday
drink at a time when whisky itself was hard to get, rather than a
connoisseur's pour.

The drink then fell out of fashion in Japan through the final decades of
the twentieth century, kept alive mainly by bartenders who had learned it
the old way <Cite ref="broadsheet"/>. Its return came from outside: as the
international craft cocktail movement began paying attention to Japanese
bars in the 2000s, it found highball technique preserved there in unusual
detail — precise ice, a fixed pour and a particular way of stirring — and
treated bartenders such as Kazunari Maguchi as specialists in a serious
drink rather than a simple one <Cite ref="broadsheet"/>. Suntory backed the
revival directly, putting highballs on tap in izakayas by 2010 and, in
2016, releasing Toki, a blend built specifically to hold its balance
against ice and soda <Cite ref="broadsheet"/>. The result is a drink
whose ingredients are British and American in origin but whose technique,
as served today, is understood internationally as specifically Japanese
<Cite ref="scmp"/>.
