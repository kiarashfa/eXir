The Sidecar's name is agreed on even where its inventor is not: several
versions of the story have a customer, usually a soldier, arriving at a Paris
bar in the sidecar of a motorcycle, and the drink made for him keeping the
name <Cite ref="chowhound"/>. Three establishments each have a documented
claim to being that bar. The Ritz Paris credits its bartender Frank Meier,
with the cognac house Rémy Martin dating the drink to the Ritz in 1923 and
Hennessy's own history placing it there as early as 1920 <Cite ref="cognac"/>.
Harry MacElhone, of Harry's New York Bar in Paris, published a Sidecar recipe
in his 1922 *ABC of Mixing Cocktails* — but that edition credits the drink not
to himself but to Pat MacGarry, a bartender at Buck's Club in London; only in
later editions did MacElhone claim to have invented it himself
<Cite ref="chowhound"/> <Cite ref="cognac"/>.

None of the three accounts can be independently confirmed over the others,
and the dates given for the Ritz version alone range across several years of
the early 1920s depending on the source citing it <Cite ref="cognac"/>. What
is fixed is the printed record: a Sidecar recipe was in circulation by 1922,
whichever bar first poured it.

The drink is also read as a simplified descendant of the Brandy Crusta, a
sugar-rimmed brandy sour built with maraschino and bitters that was served in
New Orleans decades earlier; the Sidecar keeps the sugared rim and the
citrus-and-liqueur shape while dropping the additional ingredients
<Cite ref="chowhound"/>.
