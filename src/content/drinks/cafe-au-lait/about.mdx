Coffee mixed with milk is documented in France earlier than most of the
drinks this site covers: the Marquise de Sévigné, a French aristocrat whose
letters are a standard source for the period, referred to it in her
correspondence in the seventeenth century <Cite ref="nescafe"/>. That makes
café au lait one of the oldest named milk-coffee preparations rather than a
later refinement of espresso-bar culture, and it predates the espresso
machine by more than two centuries.

The traditional composition has stayed close to that early habit: strong
coffee and hot milk combined in roughly equal parts, poured into an
oversized, handleless bowl rather than a cup, so that bread or a croissant
can be dunked directly into it at breakfast <Cite ref="nescafe"/>
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That is the detail that most separates it from a
caffè latte, which is built on espresso rather than on drip- or
press-brewed coffee <Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — café au lait's coffee is
brewed the way an ordinary pot of coffee is, then lightened with milk at
roughly its own strength, not concentrated into a shot first.

The drink's best-known regional variant is not French. New Orleans café au
lait is built on coffee cut with chicory, a substitution that dates to the
American Civil War, when a Union naval blockade of the port cut off the
city's coffee supply and chicory root was roasted and ground in to stretch
what remained <Cite ref="nescafe"/> <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The chicory
addition outlived the shortage that caused it and is now treated as the
New Orleans style in its own right, poured the same equal-parts way as the
French original.
