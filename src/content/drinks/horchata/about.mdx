The name traces to Roman *hordeata*, a barley drink, from the Latin
*hordeum* for barley, though the drink most associated with the word today
is not made from grain at all <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Tiger nuts, the tuber
Valencian horchata is actually built on, reached the Iberian peninsula
during Muslim rule beginning in the eighth century and took root
particularly well around Valencia, and by the thirteenth century local
writing already mentions "llet de xufes," tiger nut milk, as the drink's
direct ancestor <Cite ref="visitvalencia"/><Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Valencia
still produces the tiger-nut version under a protected designation of
origin today, treating it as a defining regional product rather than one
drink among many <Cite ref="visitvalencia"/>.

The name's popular origin story, in which King James I of Aragon tastes the
drink from a young Valencian woman and declares "this is not milk, this is
gold, girl" — a pun the Valencian word for horchata is said to echo — is
told locally as a charming legend rather than a documented event, and no
source treats it as established history <Cite ref="visitvalencia"/>.

Horchata's rice version is a later, separate adaptation. Spanish colonists
carried the drink to the Americas, but tiger nuts were not readily grown
across most of Mexico, and cooks there substituted rice, usually with
cinnamon and sugar, to reproduce something like the original's pale colour
and milky body with what was actually available <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
That substitution is now the version most people outside Spain mean by the
word, even though it shares no plant with the drink it was named after.
