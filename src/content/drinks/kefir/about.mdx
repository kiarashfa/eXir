The culture is older in the region than any written record of the drink.
Mountain communities of the North Caucasus are said to have fermented milk
with the grains — a living, gelatinous culture of bacteria and yeast — for
close to two millennia, traditionally in a milk-filled skin bag hung by a
doorway and knocked by anyone passing through to keep it moving
<Cite ref="matador"/>. Families guarded their own grains as a kind of
heirloom, and legend credited their origin to a gift from Muhammad, which is
why they were sometimes called grains of the prophet <Cite ref="matador"/>.
The word itself entered Russian only in the 1870s and English shortly after,
in the 1880s <Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — evidence of how late the wider world
caught up with a practice already old in its home region.

That secrecy is central to how the drink reached the rest of Russia at all.
Russian physicians in the nineteenth century, following theories tying
Bulgarian longevity to fermented milk, wanted to study kefir, but Caucasus
communities would not part with their grains <Cite ref="matador"/>. Two
cheese-factory owners, the Blandov brothers, are reported to have sent an
employee to approach a local prince for a supply; when the prince is said to
have kidnapped her instead, her escape and appeal to the court of Tsar
Nicholas II ended with the prince ordered to pay her in kefir grains
<Cite ref="matador"/>. The Blandovs began commercial production in Russia by
1908, and the drink was in wide production across the country within a few
decades <Cite ref="matador"/>.

The living culture is also what makes kefir genuinely fermented rather than
merely cultured the way yoghurt is: small-scale Russian dairies of the early
twentieth century produced kefir carrying one to two percent alcohol, a
by-product of the yeast in the grains rather than of any added spirit,
though that level of alcohol is uncommon in kefir made today
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The same grains, strained out and reused rather than
consumed, are what separate this drink from any yoghurt-based one on this
site: yoghurt is a single bacterial culture, and kefir's grains carry yeast
alongside it.
