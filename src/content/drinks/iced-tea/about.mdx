The commonly told origin puts iced tea at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair,
credited to Richard Blechynden, the India Tea Commissioner running its East
Indian Pavilion. The drink is considerably older than that. Marion Cabell
Tyree's 1879 Virginia cookbook, *Housekeeping in Old Virginia*, already
contains what is treated as the oldest sweet tea recipe in print — tea
steeped overnight, then sweetened and poured over ice glasses at serving time
<Cite ref="whatscooking"/>. A Boston cookbook of 1884 gives a black tea
version aimed at a wider American audience than the Virginia original
<Cite ref="whatscooking"/>, and the drink was already appearing on restaurant
menus at the Chicago World's Fair of 1893, over a decade before St. Louis
<Cite ref="whatscooking"/>. The 1904 fair popularised the drink far beyond
where it started rather than inventing it.

The Southern sweet version has its own separately documented history. A
Missouri gathering of Confederate veterans in 1890 served, by report, several
hundred gallons of iced tea at a single reunion, evidence that a sweetened,
chilled tea was already an established feature of Southern hospitality before
the more famous fair gave the drink a national stage <Cite ref="gpb"/>. Its
defining written recipe came later still: Henrietta Stanley Dull's 1928
*Southern Cooking* set out the strong-brew, hot-sweetened method that most
Southern kitchens still use, dissolving the sugar into the tea while it is
still hot rather than trying to stir it into a cold, already-diluted glass
<Cite ref="whatscooking"/>.

Prohibition, from 1920 to 1933, gave the unsweetened and sweetened versions
alike a further push as a sociable alternative to a cocktail, cementing iced
tea's place at the American table well before it needed a fair to explain it
<Cite ref="whatscooking"/>.
