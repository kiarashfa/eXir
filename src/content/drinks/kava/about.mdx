Kava descends from a wild pepper relative, *Piper wichmannii*, and was
domesticated into the sterile, cultivated plant known today in northern
Vanuatu roughly three thousand years ago; because the cultivated plant
cannot reproduce on its own, every kava plant since has depended on people
propagating it by hand <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. From Vanuatu it spread with
Lapita and later Austronesian voyagers east across much of the Pacific,
though it never reached New Zealand, where the climate will not support it
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Preparing it from dried root, as this page does,
is itself a documented historical adaptation rather than the original
method: fresh root pounded or chewed was standard until the eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries, when missionary opposition to kava drinking in parts
of the Pacific pushed the practice toward dried root that could be
prepared more discreetly <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

In Vanuatu the drink has long carried real social weight rather than
functioning as an everyday refreshment: traditionally reserved largely for
chiefs and significant occasions, it remains central to weddings, funerals
and the welcoming of guests, and communal kava bars called nakamals serve
as a setting for village discussion and dispute resolution
<Cite ref="vanuatutravel"/>.

Kava's effects and their risks are both real subjects of ongoing research
rather than settled facts in either direction. A group of compounds called
kavalactones is responsible for most of the plant's activity, producing a
reported calm and sociability without an accompanying loss of mental
clarity, alongside a characteristic numbing of the mouth
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Kava was restricted or banned in several countries
in the early 2000s after reports connected it to liver injury, and the
World Health Organization has since distinguished between traditional,
water-based preparation, which it judged an acceptably low health risk, and
concentrated solvent-based extracts, which it linked to a higher risk of
harm; some of those national restrictions have since been reconsidered,
while a 2017 European assessment still found the balance of benefit and
risk for medicinal use unfavourable <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The dispute is
substantially about preparation method and extraction rather than about
kava root itself.
