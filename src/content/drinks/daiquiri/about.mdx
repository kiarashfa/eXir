The name comes from Daiquirí, a beach and an iron mine near Santiago de Cuba,
itself a word of Taíno origin <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The drink is credited to
Jennings Cox, an American mining engineer working there for the Pennsylvania
Steel Company, who mixed the local white rum with lime juice and sugar
around 1898 <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="natgeo"/>.

How it left Cuba is recorded twice, and the two accounts do not compete.
William A. Chanler, a US congressman who bought iron mines in the area, is said
to have taken it to New York clubs in 1902; Rear Admiral Lucius W. Johnson, a
Navy medical officer who had drunk it in Cuba, introduced it at the Army and Navy
Club in Washington in 1909 <Cite ref="wiki"/>. A drink can arrive in two cities.

Havana then built a second drink on the same three ingredients. Constantino
Ribalaigua Vert, bartender and later owner of El Floridita, was serving a frozen
version by the 1930s — crushed ice through a blender rather than a shake, and
usually a fruit besides <Cite ref="natgeo"/>. That is the one Ernest Hemingway
drank there, and the line attributed to him about a daiquiri tasting of neither
rum nor lime nor sugar but of daiquiri belongs to the frozen drink rather than to
this one <Cite ref="natgeo"/>. The shaken version at the mines came first by
three decades, and the two have been confused ever since.
