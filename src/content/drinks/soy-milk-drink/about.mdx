The earliest known reference to soy milk in China appears around 82 AD, in
Wang Chong's *Lun Heng*, in a passing remark that makers of soymilk feared
thunder would spoil their product <Cite ref="soyinfo"/>. A popular story
credits its invention, along with tofu itself, to Liu An, a Han-dynasty
prince, in the second century BC — but the earliest version of that legend
only appears in print in the late 1500s, and even that original source
attributes tofu alone to Liu An, with soy milk added to his supposed
accomplishments by later writers with no historical basis given for the
claim <Cite ref="soyinfo"/>. It is a useful case of how a food origin story
can be both widely repeated and, on the documentary record, unsupported.

What the historical sources agree on is a split that has lasted into the
present: soy milk was drunk two distinct ways early on, sweetened as a
morning drink and unsweetened in a savoury, soup-like preparation
alongside fried dough, though exactly when and where that split developed
is not recorded <Cite ref="soyinfo"/>. Today's doujiang, sold hot from
bowls at breakfast stalls rather than bottled the way dounai, its
shelf-stable packaged cousin, is, keeps that same basic choice between
sweet and savoury alive <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. It is also the base
liquid for douhua, the soft-set tofu pudding sold at the same stalls: the
same soy milk, left liquid and drunk, or curdled and eaten with a spoon.
