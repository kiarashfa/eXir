The word cocktail was defined in print in 1806 as a mixture of spirits, sugar,
water and bitters, with no further embellishment implied <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
By the 1860s bartenders were adding curaçao, absinthe and other liqueurs to
that base, and a drinker who wanted the plainer, older style had to ask for it
specifically — which is where the name comes from. It names a request for
something already old-fashioned, not a drink invented under that description.

The commonly told origin traces it to the Pendennis Club in Louisville,
Kentucky, crediting bartender Martin Cuneo with making it for the bourbon
distiller Colonel James E. Pepper sometime between 1889 and 1895
<Cite ref="diffords"/>. The dates make the claim impossible: the *Chicago Daily
Tribune* was already writing about "old fashioned cocktails" in February 1880,
a year before the Pendennis Club was even founded <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The
story appears to originate with Albert Stevens Crockett's writing in the
1930s, decades after the drink itself, and Crockett's own account does not
describe the muddled fruit the club's version of the story is built around
<Cite ref="diffords"/>. A former Pendennis bar manager who published his own
drinks book in 1914 neither mentions fruit in the recipe nor claims the drink
for his old employer, which is the kind of silence that a genuine claim of
invention does not usually keep <Cite ref="diffords"/>.

None of this means the club invented nothing. It may have served an excellent
version and helped standardise the bourbon-and-bitters build that most bars
now serve by default. What it did not do is originate a drink that newspapers
were already naming a decade before the club opened its doors.

The earliest known printed recipe under this name appears in a Chicago
bartending manual in 1888 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>, seven years after the
Tribune's reference to the term and a year before the Pendennis story is even
set. The gap between the name in print and the story built around it is the
same shape this catalogue keeps finding in cocktail history: the drink is
older than the anecdote, and the anecdote is what survives.
