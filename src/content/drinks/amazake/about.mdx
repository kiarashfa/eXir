Amazake is old enough to appear in the *Nihon Shoki*, Japan's earliest
official chronicle, compiled in 720 <Cite ref="pmc"/>. The chronicle records
an offering of rice-and-koji liquor to Emperor Ojin around the late third
century, and a separate ceremonial drink tied to a mythological birth,
either of which is sometimes pointed to as the drink's origin
<Cite ref="shungate"/>. A Nara-period poem by Yamanoue no Okura describes
ordinary people warming themselves with lees dissolved in hot water, which
is the earliest clear trace of the sake-kasu version rather than the
koji one <Cite ref="shungate"/>.

Peddlers were already selling amazake in the streets by the Muromachi
period, and by the Edo period the drink was made much as it is today and
sold widely enough that the shogunate set a maximum price for it
<Cite ref="shungate"/><Cite ref="pmc"/>. It was drunk hot through the winter
and, counterintuitively to a modern reader, cold in summer as well, valued
as a quick source of sugar and vitamins during the hottest months before
refrigeration made other drinks easy to keep cool.

The two methods on this page are genuinely different processes rather than
a stylistic choice. Koji amazake steeps steamed rice with rice-koji at a
temperature the koji's own enzymes need to convert starch into glucose,
and it develops no alcohol at all. Sake-kasu amazake instead starts from
the solid lees pressed out of sake during brewing, which already carry a
small amount of alcohol and sake yeast; dissolved in hot water and sweetened
rather than left to ferment further, it keeps that alcohol rather than
being sweetened by any enzyme of its own <Cite ref="pmc"/>. A drink sold
simply as amazake could historically be either, and the two are not
interchangeable for anyone avoiding alcohol.
