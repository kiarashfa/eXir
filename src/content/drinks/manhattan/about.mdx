The earliest documented reference is a newspaper item from September 1882
noting that a mixture of whiskey, vermouth and bitters had come into vogue
under several names, the Manhattan among them <Cite ref="diffords"/>. Full
recipes followed in bartending guides two years later, some calling for Scotch
and others for French or Italian vermouth before rye and Italian sweet
vermouth settled as the standard pairing <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The best-known origin story credits a bartender at the Manhattan Club with
inventing the drink at a banquet held on the night of December 29, 1874, for
Samuel J. Tilden's election as governor of New York, at the request of Jennie
Jerome — later Lady Randolph Churchill and mother of Winston Churchill
<Cite ref="diffords"/>. The story does not hold up against the record: Jerome
had given birth to her son in England weeks earlier and was not in New York
for the dinner, and the Manhattan Club did not occupy the building the story
places it in until 1899, a quarter century after the party it is supposed to
describe <Cite ref="diffords"/>. A more plausible source, traced by historian
David Wondrich, is a bartender named Black — probably George Black, who ran
the Manhattan Inn on lower Broadway from 1874 until his death in 1881
<Cite ref="diffords"/>.

The whiskey has drifted more than the story. Rye was the drink's original base
and is what most of the early recipes name, though bourbon, Canadian whisky
and other blends have stood in at different points since — Canadian whisky
especially during Prohibition, when it was what actually reached the country
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The vermouth is where the drink's named variations
sit rather than the whiskey: sweet vermouth is the version usually served
under the plain name, dry vermouth in its place makes the Dry Manhattan, and
equal parts of both make the Perfect <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
