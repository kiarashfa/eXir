The name has nothing to do with the drink's ingredients and everything to
do with a borrowed word. "Toddy" comes from *tari* or *taddy*, a Hindi term
recorded from the 1610s for fermented palm sap, an unrelated alcoholic
drink native to India <Cite ref="vinepair"/>. By 1786 the English word had
already shifted meaning, recorded specifically as "a beverage made of
alcoholic liquor with hot water, sugar, and spices" — the shape of the
drink served today rather than the palm sap it was named after
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

British colonists in India are credited with carrying the idea home, where
it was adapted to the cold, damp climates of Scotland and northern England
using Scotch whisky and spices reaching Britain through the same colonial
trade <Cite ref="vinepair"/>. A separate, competing account credits the
Irish physician Robert Bentley Todd with a medical version of the drink,
made from hot brandy, canella bark and sugar syrup, prescribed to patients
rather than served in a pub <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The two stories are not
mutually exclusive: one retelling suggests physicians of the period likely
knew of the Indian drink already and folded it into their own prescriptions
rather than inventing something unrelated <Cite ref="vinepair"/>.

The drink crossed the Atlantic into colonial America, where local spirits
such as rum and brandy stood in for whisky, and by the 1830s American
newspapers were promoting hot toddies specifically as a treatment for colds
<Cite ref="vinepair"/>. That reputation as a folk remedy persists in how the
drink is still described today — for the vitamin C, the honey to soothe a
sore throat, and the alcohol's numbing effect — without either source
consulted here making a medical claim that it actually treats an illness
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.
