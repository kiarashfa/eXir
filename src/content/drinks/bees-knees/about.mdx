The Bee's Knees has no single documented inventor, and the two names most
often attached to it point in different directions. One account credits Frank
Meier, an Austrian-born bartender who became the first head bartender at the
Ritz Hotel in Paris when its Café Parisien opened in 1921 <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
A second, from a 1929 newspaper item, attributes it instead to Margaret
Brown, the American socialite later known for surviving the sinking of the
*Titanic* <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Neither claim is corroborated by a recipe in
print at the time, which is why the drink is usually described as being of
unclear origin rather than assigned to either name outright.

What is not in dispute is the period. The name is Prohibition-era slang for
"the best," and the drink belongs to American Prohibition, when gin was the
spirit most readily available to make at home and lemon and honey were
ordinary kitchen goods rather than a bar's stock <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The drink has also had a documented modern revival. Barr Hill, a Vermont
gin distillery, began an annual "Bee's Knees Week" in 2017 built around
pollinator conservation, and a 2023 *New York Times* article credited that
promotion with part of the cocktail's renewed popularity <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
The honey syrup that gives the drink its name is the one part of the recipe
that has never been in question, whatever the disagreement about who first
poured it.
