A lemon-and-mint drink is recorded in the Middle East well before its modern
Israeli name existed. A 13th-century recipe by the Andalusian scholar Ibn
al-Baytar calls for sugar, lemon juice, mint and water, and other regional
accounts trace sweetened lemon drinks to medieval Cairo and to Ottoman
Turkey and Syria <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The combination is common enough across
the region that several countries claim it as their own, without a single
documented point of origin <Cite ref="foodrepublic"/>.

The name limonana, and much of the drink's current popularity in Israel,
comes from somewhere much more recent. In the 1990s, the advertising agency
Fogel-Levin ran a bus campaign for a product called Limonana, featuring
celebrities promoting it — before the drink existed as an actual product
<Cite ref="foodrepublic"/>. The name itself is a portmanteau of the Hebrew
and Arabic words for lemon and mint <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Customers began
asking for the drink by that name at restaurants and market stalls, and once
the demand was real, restaurants and soft-drink makers began making an
actual limonana to match it <Cite ref="foodrepublic"/>.

The blended, semi-frozen style served today — lemon juice and mint blended
directly with ice rather than poured over it — is now closely associated
with Israel specifically, sometimes informally called the country's national
drink, though the designation carries no official status
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.
