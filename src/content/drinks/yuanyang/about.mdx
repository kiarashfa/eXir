The drink takes its name from the mandarin duck, a species in which the male
and female look strikingly unlike each other but which mates for life and is
long used in Chinese culture as a symbol of a devoted pair
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Coffee and milk tea are an equally unlike pairing,
and the name reads as much as a joke about the two ingredients as it does a
compliment.

It belongs to the dai pai dong and cha chaan teng tradition — the open-air
stalls and fast, informal cafés that grew up around Hong Kong's working
population — rather than to any tea house or coffee shop of the kind that
serve the drink's two components separately <Cite ref="scmp"/>. Lan Fong Yuen,
a long-running cha chaan teng, claims to have invented it in 1952, though that
specific claim is not independently verified, unlike the same restaurant's
better-documented claim to "silk-stocking" milk tea, its close relative
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What is agreed is simpler and less dependent on any
one shop: combining leftover coffee and tea was a practical way to stretch two
drinks that manual labourers wanted and pure tea, at the time, was priced out
of reach for <Cite ref="scmp"/>.

Hong Kong's Leisure and Cultural Services Department, in describing the
drink for its own heritage documentation, states the standard mixture as
three parts coffee to seven parts Hong Kong–style milk tea
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — the ratio this page follows, rather than an equal
split of the two. The milk tea itself, pulled hard through a cloth filter and
finished with evaporated rather than fresh milk, is unchanged from how it is
served on its own; coffee is added to it, not the other way round.
