Coconut water needs no separate invention story: it is what is already
inside a young *Cocos nucifera* fruit, and wherever the palm grows near
people — coastal India, Southeast Asia, the Pacific islands, the Brazilian
coast, the Caribbean and much of tropical Africa — it has long been drunk
straight from the shell rather than prepared as a dish <Cite ref="diffordsguide"/>.
Street vendors across those regions are noted for the skill of opening a
coconut in a few strokes of a machete or by boring a single hole through the
husk, serving the water on the spot rather than decanting it
<Cite ref="diffordsguide"/>.

Its traditional standing was medicinal as much as refreshing. Coconut water
has long been offered in the tropics to people suffering from diarrhoea, and
the clearest documented use of it as an emergency medical fluid comes from
the Second World War, when dehydrated British and Japanese patients are
recorded as having been given it intravenously in place of saline solution
that had run short <Cite ref="diffordsguide"/>. That account is notable
enough to be repeated in food writing, though it describes a wartime
improvisation rather than a standard clinical practice.

Regional use since has diversified around the same fruit without displacing
coconut water's own simple role: coconut milk, the pressed liquid from
grated mature flesh, became central to curries and desserts across South and
Southeast Asia, while the water itself stayed a beach and street drink from
Brazil to the Pacific, served fresh and unprocessed rather than cooked into
anything <Cite ref="copracoconuts"/>. The two liquids share a fruit but not
a preparation, which is also why they are not interchangeable in a recipe
that specifically calls for one or the other.
