The name comes from Greek rather than any language closer to where the plant
is drunk today: *khamaimēlon*, literally "earth apple," a description of its
low growth and its apple-like scent, entered English by way of French and
Latin and has been in use since the thirteenth century <Cite ref="wiki-chamomile"/>.
The flower it describes was in use as a remedy long before the word existed —
reviews of its traditional applications place it in ancient Egypt, Greece and
Rome without dating the practice more precisely than that
<Cite ref="pmc-review"/>.

Its documented history outside Europe is more exact. In Uyghur medicine, a
tenth-century text known as the Zhu Medical Canon records the plant under the
name Bamu Nai and prescribes it for chronic headache, constipation, poor
sweating and joint swelling <Cite ref="pmc-review"/> — a use for the flower's
anti-inflammatory and calming properties that has changed remarkably little
in the intervening centuries, even as the specific ailments treated with it
have narrowed toward digestion and sleep in most of the world it is drunk in
now.

What has stayed constant across all of these traditions is the form: the
whole dried flower head steeped loose in hot water, taken without milk and
usually without sweetening, valued for an aroma rather than a strength the
way a black tea is. Nothing about the modern teabag version — the same
flower, ground finer and enclosed in paper — changes that basic shape. It
remains one of the few infusions in wide use today that a Roman herbalist
would recognise by nose alone.
