The pisco sour is generally traced to Victor Vaughen Morris, an American who
moved to Peru in 1904, settled in Lima in 1915 and opened Morris' Bar there
the following year <Cite ref="wiki"/>. He is credited with building the drink
as a pisco-based variation on the whiskey sour, though the earliest
advertisement naming it dates only to 1920, and a 1903 Peruvian cookbook has
since turned up a pisco punch made with egg white that predates his bar
entirely, leaving open whether Morris invented the drink or refined one
already circulating in Lima <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The egg white, Angostura
bitters and the frothy head associated with the drink today are credited to
Mario Bruiget, a Peruvian bartender who worked under Morris from 1924
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Peru and Chile have disputed the drink, and the spirit it is built on, for
decades <Cite ref="takeout"/>. Chile's rival claim rests on a local story
crediting an English steward named Elliot Stubb, a story a later researcher
traced to newspaper reports that actually describe a whiskey sour rather than
a pisco one <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The countries also disagree over pisco
itself: Peru holds the European Union's protected designation of origin for
the name, and manuscripts examined in 2024 documenting sixteenth- and
seventeenth-century production and export were read as supporting Peru's
claim to the spirit, though Chile continues to produce and sell pisco of its
own under its own rules <Cite ref="takeout"/>.

What is not disputed is that the two countries now make different drinks
under the same name. Peru's version takes egg white and bitters; Chile's
does not, leaning instead on a drier, more citrus-forward balance of pisco,
lime and sugar <Cite ref="takeout"/>.
