Kompot is documented from as early as the fifteenth century <Cite ref="flavors196"/>,
and it began as a preservation method rather than a drink made for its own
sake. In Russia's agrarian communities, fruit that would otherwise spoil
before winter was boiled down with sugar and kept in sealed clay jars or
wooden barrels, the sugar concentration acting as the preservative that let
a family taste summer fruit months after the harvest had ended
<Cite ref="howtorussia"/>. The liquid that resulted from that process — not
the fruit itself — became the drink, which is why kompot is defined by what
is left in the pot after the fruit has given up its flavour rather than by
the fruit being eaten as food.

The technique spread beyond Russia into Poland, Armenia and the Balkans,
adapting to whatever fruit was regionally abundant rather than fixing on one
recipe <Cite ref="flavors196"/>. Cherries, berries, apples, plums, apricots
and rhubarb are all named as traditional choices, and the recipe is
consistently described as flexible rather than standardised around any
single fruit <Cite ref="flavors196"/> — a bird cherry kompot, sharper and
more astringent than one made with sweet cherries or berries, sits within
that same open tradition rather than outside it.

Kompot's serving context reflects its origin as a preserved, made-ahead
food rather than something mixed to order: it is brought out at family
gatherings and festivals in large communal bowls, served warm in winter and
chilled in summer, and its presence at the table is treated as a gesture of
hospitality in its own right <Cite ref="howtorussia"/>. That is also why it
sits alongside kvass as one of the drinks most associated with everyday
Russian domestic life rather than with any single occasion.
