A sweet rice-flour drink resembling cendol appears in the *Kakawin
Kresnayana*, a twelfth-century Javanese manuscript written by Mpu Triguna
in the Kediri Kingdom of East Java, which is the earliest documentary trace
historians point to for the drink <Cite ref="wikipedia"/><Cite ref="seasia"/>.
The Indonesian historian Fadly Rahman has pointed to that manuscript
specifically in response to a 2018 international list that credited
Singapore as cendol's origin, and a chef and food writer, William Wongso,
has separately described dawet, the drink's closest Javanese relative, as
having existed for three to four centuries under that name <Cite ref="seasia"/>.
The earliest written recipe in a colonial source appears later still, in
an 1866 Dutch-language East Indies cookbook, and an 1859 Makassarese-Dutch
dictionary already glosses a version of the word for the rice or sago
paste pressed through a mould to make the jelly strands <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The word cendol itself is generally traced to the Malay *jendol*, meaning a
bump or bulge, describing the look of the short jelly strands rather than
any place name <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That the drink and its close
relatives are documented not only among Javanese and Sundanese cooks but
also among Malay, Minangkabau, Bugis, Vietnamese, Khmer, Thai and Burmese
communities suggests a shared regional food tradition that predates any one
country's modern borders, which is part of why the drink is now claimed by
several Southeast Asian nations at once <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Each has
since developed its own version — Malaysia's typically adds sweetened red
beans, for instance — while agreeing on the same core of rice-flour jelly,
coconut milk and palm sugar over ice <Cite ref="seasia"/><Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
