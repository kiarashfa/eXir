Ethiopia is the plant's undisputed botanical home, but the ceremony built
around it is much harder to date than the popular story about it suggests.
The widely repeated tale of a goatherd named Kaldi, whose goats grew
energetic after eating coffee cherries, is folklore rather than documented
history: its earliest known written appearance is in a Latin text from 1671,
centuries after coffee drinking is independently attested <Cite ref="jayarr"/>.
The first solid written record of coffee actually being brewed and drunk
comes not from Ethiopia but from fifteenth-century Yemen <Cite ref="jayarr"/>.

What the ceremony itself preserves is a method rather than an origin date:
green beans washed and roasted in a long-handled pan over a small fire,
shaken constantly so they colour evenly rather than scorch, then pounded to
a powder with a mortar and pestle <Cite ref="compassion"/>. The grounds go
into a jebena, a round-bodied clay pot with a long neck, filled with water and
brought to the boil directly over the fire <Cite ref="compassion"/>. Regional
variants of the pot's shape track how the coffee itself is served — the
Mankira jebena used in Kafa carries a modified spout because Kafa coffee is
traditionally finished with a floating layer of butter, which the standard
design would let escape while pouring <Cite ref="jayarr"/>.

The coffee is poured in three successive rounds from the same grounds, each
weaker than the last: abol, tona and a closing round usually called baraka or
bereka. The structure is understood locally as mirroring conversation itself
— an opening round for greeting, a middle round where the actual exchange
happens, and a closing round that seals it <Cite ref="jayarr"/>. Incense is
often burned alongside the roasting, and the ceremony is a social occasion
built around the coffee rather than a fast way of making it — closer in
spirit to the tea ceremonies of East Asia than to a quick cup before work.
