The phrase "caffè latte" is old enough to predate the espresso machine
entirely. The American writer William Dean Howells recorded being served one
during a steamship crossing from Trieste to Venice in 1867, but the drink he
described was closer to a café au lait than to what the name means today,
since nothing in it could have been espresso-based milk foam decades before
the machinery existed <Cite ref="dailymeal"/>. That makes it a case where the
name is real and old and the drink it now refers to is neither.

The modern espresso-based latte is usually credited to Lino Meiorin, an
Italian-trained barista who co-owned Caffe Mediterraneum in Berkeley,
California. By his own account, recorded later in Kenneth Davids's coffee
writing, he built the drink in 1959 after customers kept finding his
cappuccinos too small and too strong and asking for more milk — he answered
by pouring the same shot into a larger cup with proportionally more steamed
milk and naming it after the Italian word for milk <Cite ref="dailycoffeenews"/>.
Caffe Mediterraneum still stakes its identity on the claim.

Both sources treat this as the popularising account rather than a settled
first: it is unlikely Meiorin was the only barista anywhere lengthening
espresso with milk, and the honest position is that he standardised and
named a drink already implicit in the ingredients on hand rather than
inventing a technique from nothing <Cite ref="dailymeal"/>. What is not in
dispute is that the drink spread from American espresso bars outward, not
from Italy, however Italian its name reads.
