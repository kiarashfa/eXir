Coffee reached Istanbul under the Ottomans, and a legal document already
mentions a dedicated coffee chamber in the household of the admiral Barbaros
Hayreddin Pasha by 1539 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The city's first coffeehouses
followed within about fifteen years, opened by two merchants from Aleppo and
Damascus, and their number is said to have passed six hundred not long after
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The method served in them is the one still used: coffee
ground close to a powder, cooked directly in water in a small handled pot, with
nothing filtered out before it reaches the cup.

UNESCO inscribed Turkish coffee culture and tradition on its Representative
List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013, and its own
description of the method matches the traditional one closely — finely ground
beans brewed slowly with water and sugar in the pot until a foam forms on top
<Cite ref="unesco"/>. The listing is explicit that the designation covers more
than the recipe: it names the drink's role in hospitality, in engagement
ceremonies, and in a tradition of reading the settled grounds after the cup is
finished, alongside the brewing itself <Cite ref="unesco"/>.

The same cezve method, under different national names, is shared across a
region that once sat inside or beside the Ottoman world — it is served as
Greek coffee in Greece and by comparable names elsewhere in the Balkans and
the Levant, with the pot and the technique effectively unchanged
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What distinguishes the method from every other coffee
on this site is that nothing about it is filtered or pressurised: the grounds
are cooked directly into the water they are served in, and the foam raised in
stages during that cooking, not the crema of a pressurised shot, is the mark
of a cup made correctly.
