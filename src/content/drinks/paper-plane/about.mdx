Sam Ross built the drink in 2008 at the request of his former colleague Toby
Maloney, who wanted a new cocktail for Maloney's own bar, The Violet Hour, in
Chicago <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="punch"/>. Ross conceived it as an
equal-parts riff on the Last Word, the pre-Prohibition cocktail Seattle
bartender Murray Stenson had recently returned to circulation, keeping that
drink's four-way structure but rebuilding it around bourbon, an Italian
bitter aperitivo, a grape-based amaro and lemon rather than gin, herbal
liqueur, maraschino and lime <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="punch"/>.

The recipe changed within days of being written. Ross's first version used
Campari; tasting it again, he found it too bitter and not sweet enough, and
swapped in Aperol instead, arriving at the bourbon, Aperol, Amaro Nonino and
lemon combination served today <Cite ref="punch"/>. The name comes from the
M.I.A. song "Paper Planes," which Ross had on repeat while working out the
recipe <Cite ref="wiki"/>; when Maloney first put it on his menu he listed it
as "paper airplane," having misheard Ross's voicemail <Cite ref="punch"/>.

Ross began serving his own version at Milk & Honey and Little Branch in New
York at the same time it appeared in Chicago <Cite ref="punch"/>. It spread
quickly enough to draw its own imitators, including Joaquín Simó's mezcal
riff the Naked and Famous, and in 2020 the International Bartenders
Association added it to its official list of cocktails under the "New Era
Drinks" category — recognition inside little more than a decade of the
drink's invention <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="punch"/>.
