The earliest documented lemonade, called qatarmizat, is recorded among
Cairo's medieval Jewish community between the 10th and 13th centuries, made
from lemon juice sweetened with sugar or honey <Cite ref="classbar"/>
<Cite ref="historyoasis"/>. Lemons and sugar both reached Europe as costly
imports, which kept the drink a luxury there for centuries; 18th-century
Europeans paid a premium for a violet-scented version made from imported
Sicilian lemons and Caribbean sugar <Cite ref="historyoasis"/>.

Paris is where lemonade became a genuine street trade. Vendors sold it from
tanks carried on their backs from the 1630s, and by 1676 the city had
chartered the Compagnie de Limonadiers, a guild that regulated the drink's
proportions, inspected the vendors' tanks and fixed its price by social
rank <Cite ref="historyoasis"/>. The first English literary reference to
lemonade dates to Thomas Killigrew's 1663 play *The Parson's Wedding*
<Cite ref="classbar"/>.

The still, cloudy style served by the glass and the clear, carbonated style
sold in bottles are the same base drink at different points in its history
rather than separate inventions. The earliest written reference to a
carbonated lemonade dates to 1833, and London had more than fifty
manufacturers of it within the following decade <Cite ref="classbar"/>.
William Terrington's 1869 guide *Cooling Cups and Dainty Drinks* records the
period method of rubbing lemon zest into sugar before combining it with
juice and carbonated water <Cite ref="classbar"/>.
