Victor Bergeron, known as Trader Vic, said he invented the drink in 1944 at
his restaurant in Oakland, California, from aged Jamaican rum, orange
curaçao, orgeat, rock candy syrup and fresh lime <Cite ref="umt"/>. By his own
account, Tahitian friends visiting the bar tasted it and said "Mai Tai — roa
ae," meaning roughly "out of this world, the best," which he took as the
name <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="umt"/>.

Ernest Beaumont-Gantt, known as Don the Beachcomber, disputed this. He had
served a drink called the Q.B. Cooler at his own competing Los Angeles bar as
early as 1933, and later argued the Mai Tai was built on it <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
The two drinks are not identical — Beach's recipe is generally described as
the more complex of the pair — and accounts differ on how closely the two
actually taste alike <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The case for Beach's priority rests
substantially on an account from his widow, Phoebe, rather than on a
contemporary written record, while Bergeron's own claim is undercut by the
absence of any Mai Tai in his own bar guides published in 1946 and 1947
<Cite ref="umt"/>.

The dispute reached a courtroom only indirectly. In 1970, a citrus company
that had licensed the Don the Beachcomber name attempted to trademark a Mai
Tai mix, and Trader Vic's sued over the right to the name; the case settled
that year in Bergeron's favour, backed by a signed affidavit from Carrie
Guild, one of the Tahitian guests he credited with naming the drink
<Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="umt"/>. The settlement fixed who could sell a
Mai Tai commercially. It did not, and does not, settle who mixed the first
one.
