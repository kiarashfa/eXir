Constantino Ribalaigua Vert, a Catalan immigrant known in Havana as
"Constante," began tending bar at the restaurant then called La Florida in
1914 and bought the business in 1918, later renaming it El Floridita
<Cite ref="floridita"/> <Cite ref="kingdom"/>. The shaken Daiquiri, credited to
an American mining engineer working near Santiago de Cuba around 1898, was
already established by the time Constante took over the bar.

In the early 1930s, Constante adopted the newly available electric blender and
used it to turn the same rum, lime and sugar into an iced, textured drink
rather than a shaken and strained one <Cite ref="floridita"/> <Cite ref="kingdom"/>.
The frozen Daiquiri became the drink most associated with him, enough that El
Floridita still bills itself as "la cuna del daiquiri," the cradle of the
Daiquiri <Cite ref="floridita"/>. Its ratios and lime-forward balance carried
over from the shaken original; what changed was entirely the ice and the
machine that could crush it into the drink rather than around it.

Ernest Hemingway drank at El Floridita through the 1930s and into the early
1940s, keeping a room nearby at the Hotel Ambos Mundos for several of those
years and returning from his home outside Havana afterward <Cite ref="floridita"/>.
He was one of a group of regulars, including Graham Greene, drawn to
Constante's bar in part because it drew a more local clientele than the
tourist-oriented Sloppy Joe's <Cite ref="kingdom"/>. The Daiquiri he drank at
El Floridita, and the one the bar's own bronze statue of him now
commemorates, was Constante's frozen version rather than the shaken original
from three decades earlier <Cite ref="floridita"/>.
