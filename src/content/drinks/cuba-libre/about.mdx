The name translates to "free Cuba" and echoes the rallying cry of the Cuban
independence movement against Spain <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. American troops
occupied Cuba after the Spanish-American War of 1898, and bottled Coca-Cola
reached the island shortly afterwards, which is the window every version of
the drink's origin story is set in <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Beyond that, the record is thin. The most repeated story has American
soldiers in Havana mixing Cuban rum with the newly arrived Coca-Cola and
toasting "¡Por Cuba libre!" <Cite ref="swissstandard"/>, but no contemporary
account of the toast, the soldiers involved or the bar it supposedly
happened in has been produced, and the story is set right at the edge of
when Coca-Cola could plausibly have been on the island at all
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. It is best treated as an origin legend rather than
a documented event.

What is not in dispute is the drink itself: white rum, cola and lime, built
over ice, is one of the simplest members of the highball family and one of
the reasons cola sells as much of it as it does. The distinction from a
plain rum and Coke is the lime — squeezed in and often dropped in as well —
which a Cuba Libre specifies and a Rum and Coke does not.
