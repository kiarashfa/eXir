The most repeated story traces the Mojito to "El Draque," a medicinal mix of
raw cane spirit, sugar, lime and mint said to be named for the English
privateer Francis Drake after a 16th-century visit to Cuba
<Cite ref="diffords"/>. The story does not hold up well: England had no
Caribbean sugar trade in the 1590s to have produced the spirit the legend
requires, which is a specific and checkable problem with the dates rather
than a matter of taste <Cite ref="whetstone"/>. A drink called the Drake is
better documented later — a Havana writer recorded drinking one daily during
the 1833 cholera epidemic for its supposed medicinal value
<Cite ref="diffords"/>.

The rum version is tied to the Bacardi company, founded by Don Facundo
Bacardí Massó, whose rum reportedly replaced the older cane spirit in the
Drake recipe; a 1940 account states plainly that once rum replaced
aguardiente, the Drake was called a Mojito <Cite ref="diffords"/>. The
earliest printed recipe under a related name, "Mojo Criollo," dates to 1927
<Cite ref="diffords"/>.

The name's own origin is unsettled. One account derives it from *mojar*, to
wet; another traces it to *mojo*, a term connected to the enslaved
plantation workers credited with the drink's origin rather than to Drake at
all <Cite ref="whetstone"/>. The often-told claim that Ernest Hemingway
favoured the drink at a specific Havana bar has no documentary support in
his own published writing <Cite ref="diffords"/>.
