*Hibiscus sabdariffa*, known locally as bissap, was domesticated in West
Africa, in the region of the Western Sudan, and has been grown and drunk
there for far longer than it has existed anywhere else <Cite ref="wiki-hibiscus"/>.
In Senegal the infusion made from its dried calyces is treated as the
country's national drink <Cite ref="teashop"/>, sold from roadside stands and
made at home in large batches rather than by the cup. Neighbouring countries
along the same stretch of West Africa know the same plant under their own
names — wonjo in Senegal itself, zobo in Nigeria, sobolo in Ghana — before the
flower's spread further afield gave the wider world karkadeh in Egypt and the
Middle East and agua de jamaica in Mexico <Cite ref="196flavors"/>.

The West African version is generally the strongest and sweetest of that
family: a heavier hand with the sugar than the Mexican or Egyptian
preparations, and a habit of flavouring the steep itself rather than leaving
the calyx to stand alone, with mint or a few drops of orange flower water
folded in alongside the sugar. It is drunk both hot and cold, though the
cold, heavily iced version associated with market stalls and street vendors
is the one that travels — bottled and sold under the bissap name in West
African communities well beyond the region it came from.

None of this makes bissap the parent drink of its relatives elsewhere in the
world. The plant moved along more than one route out of Africa, and the
West African infusion, the Mexican agua fresca and the Middle Eastern karkadeh
are better understood as three regional treatments of one migratory flower
than as a single tradition copied outward from Senegal.
