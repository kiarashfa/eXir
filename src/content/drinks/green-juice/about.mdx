Fresh vegetable juicing as a health practice in the United States traces to
Norman W. Walker, who built the first commercial juicer, the Norwalk, and
promoted a diet built around raw juice and vegetables that he called "Living
Food" <Cite ref="vice"/>. Juicing entered wider American popularity in the
early 1970s, credited to three figures working roughly in parallel: Walker,
Gayelord Hauser and Jay Kordich <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The fitness personality
Jack LaLanne extended the practice into mainstream exercise culture with his
own Power Juicer, launched in the same period <Cite ref="vice"/>.

The specific green, vegetable-forward juice now sold under that name is a
later, early-2000s development, tied to a wave of cold-pressed juice bars
such as Organic Avenue, Liquiteria and Juice Press that opened in New York
starting in the early 2000s <Cite ref="vice"/>. These shops sold green juice
as part of multi-day cleanses at a significant premium, and celebrity
endorsement helped drive the trend into mainstream wellness culture over the
following decade <Cite ref="vice"/>. A 2011 documentary, *Fat, Sick and
Nearly Dead*, is credited with driving a further surge in juicer sales and
home juicing more broadly <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

By the early 2010s, nutritionists and journalists had begun directly
challenging the detoxification claims made for juice cleanses, and the
scientific evidence for them did not hold up; one of the trend's flagship
juice bars, Organic Avenue, filed for bankruptcy in 2015 before later
reopening <Cite ref="vice"/>. The plain, unsweetened pressed juice itself —
distinct from the cleanse marketing built around it — has remained a
standard item on juice bar menus regardless.
