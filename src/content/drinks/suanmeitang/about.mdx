Drinks of this general kind are traced back at least as far as the Song
dynasty, with a variant called "white suanmeitang" recorded during the
following Yuan dynasty <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The version closer to what
is drunk today is a Qing dynasty development, said to have been prepared at
the request of the Qianlong Emperor in the early eighteenth century, when
it was a drink confined to the imperial court rather than something sold on
the street <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. It moved beyond the palace only later,
credited in one account to a Hebei merchant whose Xinyuanzhai brand carried
it into wider commercial sale, and it stayed a specialty item made in shops
rather than a mass product until companies began producing it industrially
in the 1980s <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The drink is built from smoked plums simmered with hawthorn and dried
citrus peel, sometimes with liquorice root, and finished with sweet
osmanthus flowers, typically sweetened with rock sugar <Cite ref="peoplesdaily"/>.
It is drunk cold and is one of the most common summer drinks across China
today, sold from carts and shopfronts as a way to cut through summer heat,
and it carries a long-standing folk reputation for settling the stomach and
easing the ache that follows hard exercise, though these are traditional
beliefs about the drink rather than established medical claims
<Cite ref="peoplesdaily"/>.
