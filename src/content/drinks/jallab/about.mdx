Jallab is made from date and grape molasses and rosewater, diluted with
water and served over crushed ice with raisins and pine nuts floating on the
surface <Cite ref="wiki"/>. It descends from dibis, also called halek, an
older date and grape syrup tradition associated with Iraq and with Jewish
communities of Iraq and India <Cite ref="flavors"/>. In its traditional
form, dates were soaked and boiled, the liquid filtered through cheesecloth,
and then concentrated either by simmering it down or by leaving it in trays
in the sun until it thickened into a dense syrup <Cite ref="flavors"/>. That
slow, sun-concentrated syrup is largely a home and small-producer method
today; most jallab syrup sold commercially is made by faster, industrial
reduction <Cite ref="flavors"/>.

The drink is closely tied to Ramadan in the Levant, where it is a common
drink for breaking the daily fast <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Its base syrup carries
a separate religious use outside that context: some Bene Israel and Baghdadi
Jewish communities used dibis in place of sugar in their Passover haroset,
the sweet paste eaten at the seder, since sugar was historically an
expensive import in the region while dates and grapes were not
<Cite ref="flavors"/>.

Regional variants depart from the plain date-and-rosewater style in
different directions: an Iraqi version, shalgham helu, preserves turnips
directly in the syrup, while versions made in India are often scented with
star anise rather than rosewater alone <Cite ref="flavors"/>. Some
preparations are also finished by smoking the syrup with Arabic incense
before serving <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
