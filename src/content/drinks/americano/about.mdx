Gaspare Campari opened a bar in Milan's Galleria Vittorio Emanuele in 1867 to
sell the bitter liqueur he had invented around 1860 <Cite ref="diffords"/>. An
early house drink there paired Campari from Milan with sweet vermouth from
Turin and was known by the two cities' names, Milano-Torino
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Soda water was added later, turning a short bitter
aperitivo into the longer drink served today.

The name it carries now dates to the 1920s, when American tourists visiting
Europe to drink through Prohibition took to it in large numbers and the bar
trade started calling it the Americano after its clientele rather than its
ingredients <Cite ref="diffords"/>. It has no connection to the coffee that
shares the name, which is a much later Italian-American invention built
around espresso rather than Campari.

The drink is a direct ancestor of the Negroni: adding gin to an Americano,
the story goes, is how that later drink came about. Both pages carry
Campari and sweet vermouth in the same equal measure; the Americano's third
part is soda rather than a spirit, which is the whole of the difference
between a bitter highball and a stirred, spirit-forward one.
