The fizz was first recorded in print by Jerry Thomas in 1867, as spirit,
lemon, sweetener and seltzer <Cite ref="punch"/>. Gin was one of several base
spirits the format was built around from the start, and the resulting drink
spread widely enough in the United States between 1900 and the 1940s that it
became known as a hometown specialty of New Orleans, where bars are said to
have kept teams of bartenders shaking nothing else <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The format is defined against its nearest relative, the Collins: the two
share a near-identical build of spirit, citrus and sugar topped with soda,
and differ mainly in that the fizz is shaken before straining and drunk at
once, while the Collins is built directly over ice and left to sit
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. The distinction was enough to earn the fizz its own
reputation as a hangover remedy before the Bloody Mary took over that role
<Cite ref="punch"/>.

Several named variants branch from the same base by changing one ingredient:
a Silver Fizz adds egg white, a Golden Fizz an egg yolk, and a Royal Fizz the
whole egg <Cite ref="punch"/>. The best known and most elaborate is a separate
drink built on the same idea. Henry C. Ramos created his version, cream,
orange flower water, egg white and all, at the Imperial Cabinet Saloon in New
Orleans in 1888 <Cite ref="wiki"/>. It became popular enough before
Prohibition that the Imperial reportedly kept more than twenty bartenders
shaking it in relay, since the drink's own long shake time made a single
bartender too slow to keep up with demand <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The Roosevelt
Hotel later revived and trademarked that recipe in 1935, after its own
bartender was sent to teach it to bar staff in New York <Cite ref="wiki"/>,
and it is documented today as its own drink rather than a version of this
one.
