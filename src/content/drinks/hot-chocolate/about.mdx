Cacao was drunk in Mesoamerica for centuries before Europe encountered it. The
Maya were preparing cacao drinks by roughly 2,500 to 3,000 years ago, and the
Aztecs later treated the drink as a marker of status, holding that it was a
bad omen for anyone of low standing to drink it <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What
reached Spain from the conquest was bitter and unsweetened, seasoned with
chilli rather than sugar, and Spanish drinkers reworked it with sugar and
spices such as cinnamon to suit their own taste <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
Chocolate's spread through the rest of Europe is credited in part to royal
marriage, with Spain's monopoly on the trade said to have loosened as the
drink travelled to the French court <Cite ref="mexicolore"/>.

England came to it later, through fashionable chocolate houses that opened
from the late 1650s, serving a drink still made with water and spice rather
than milk <Cite ref="mexicolore"/>. Milk chocolate as a drink predates its
most famous name: the physician and collector Hans Sloane is popularly
credited with adding milk to chocolate after time spent in Jamaica in the
1680s, though Henry Stubbe had already described chocolate served with milk
in London by 1662, before Sloane's association with the drink took hold
<Cite ref="mexicolore"/>. By the eighteenth century hot chocolate was
established as a luxury drunk by the European nobility, distinct from the
coffee served alongside it in the same houses <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The two versions on this page reflect a real difference in kind rather than
a matter of proportion. One is made from solid chocolate, melted directly
into milk and carrying the cocoa butter that chocolate contains; the other is
made from cocoa powder, which has had most of that fat pressed out of it and
gives a thinner, quicker drink usually called cocoa rather than chocolate in
English <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Both descend from the same Mesoamerican
drink; they diverge only at the point where the cacao itself is processed.
