The drink is credited to the London bartender Dick Bradsell, working at the
Soho Brasserie in the early 1980s, with 1983 the year most often given
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Bradsell's own account was that the coffee machine
sat next to his station and coffee was on his mind; the more often repeated
version of the story has a customer, remembered only as a young model, asking
for a drink that would "wake me up, then fuck me up," a line Bradsell
recounted for the rest of his career without ever naming her
<Cite ref="punch"/>.

The original drink was simpler and differently named. Bradsell called it a
Vodka Espresso, built from vodka and a fresh shot with a little sugar syrup,
served over ice with three coffee beans on top and containing no coffee
liqueur at all <Cite ref="punch"/>. The now-standard measure of coffee
liqueur alongside the vodka and espresso was added later, and the drink did
not take on its current name, nor its usual shaken-and-strained-up service,
until Bradsell was working at Match EC1 in the late 1990s, during the general
fashion for calling anything served in that glass a "martini"
<Cite ref="punch"/>.

That name is a genuine mismatch — there is no gin or vermouth in it, and
Bradsell is on record finding the name faintly absurd even as it stuck
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What has stayed constant since the Soho Brasserie is
the technique the name obscures: a shot pulled hot and shaken immediately,
hard enough to beat its own crema into the drink's foam, which is the reason
it is built to order around the espresso rather than a syrup or a cold-brewed
substitute.
