Noon chai is usually traced to trade along the Silk Road, with one account
holding that it reached Kashmir from Yarkand, in what later became Xinjiang
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. It belongs to a wider family of salted milk teas found
across Central Asia, including Uyghur etkanchay and Mongolian suutei tsai,
and the salt itself is explained in the same terms in all of them: at high
altitude it was drunk as much as an electrolyte as a beverage
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The colour is the drink's most distinctive feature and it is produced,
rather than added. Green tea leaves are boiled for an extended period —
accounts differ on exactly how long, from roughly twenty minutes to closer
to an hour — which draws colour-changing compounds out of the leaf; a small
amount of bicarbonate of soda pushes the liquid alkaline and accelerates
that colour shift toward a deep burgundy <Cite ref="recipe52"/>
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. Cold water is then stirred in to fix the colour before
milk goes in, and it is the milk's proteins reacting with those same
compounds that turn the liquid pink <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Getting a full
froth traditionally meant ladling the hot milky tea back and forth between
two vessels by hand, a step said to take hours to do properly
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The tea is conventionally unsweetened, though sugar is common in
commercial versions and Pakistani households often serve it with sugar and
nuts on the side rather than stirred in <Cite ref="wiki"/>. In Kashmir it
is drunk several times a day, alongside bread such as kulcha, and it is
particularly associated with the Bakarwal pastoralists who carry the same
recipe with them on seasonal migration <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
