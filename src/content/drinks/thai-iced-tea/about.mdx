Tea reached Thailand through Chinese merchants who settled around its
trading ports, and it took hold domestically during the reigns of King
Rama IV and King Rama V in the mid-to-late nineteenth century
<Cite ref="lionbrand"/>. The traditional brew built on that base by adding
local aromatics — accounts differ on the exact list, but star anise,
tamarind and orange blossom water recur across most of them
<Cite ref="lionbrand"/> <Cite ref="teasco"/>.

The drink's now-familiar creamy form is a later, mid-twentieth-century
development, associated with the period of Westernising reform under
Field Marshal Plaek Phibunsongkhram, when sweetened condensed and
evaporated milk became standard additions to the spiced tea
<Cite ref="lionbrand"/>. Vendor and restaurant culture pushed the drink
further in that direction, and pre-blended, pre-mixed Thai tea powder made
it fast and consistent enough to become a fixture of street stalls and
restaurants alike <Cite ref="teasco"/>.

The bright orange colour most people associate with the drink is not a
product of the tea or the spice at all. Naturally brewed strong black tea
with milk comes out tan or caramel-coloured, and the orange seen today
comes from added colouring, for reasons that are themselves disputed: one
account holds that vendors coloured rebrewed, fading tea leaves to
disguise the weaker second steep, while another holds that colouring was
introduced simply to tell Thai iced tea apart from Thai iced coffee, a
similarly milky drink of nearly the same shade <Cite ref="lionbrand"/>.
Either way, the dye changes the drink's appearance and nothing about its
flavour <Cite ref="teasco"/>.
