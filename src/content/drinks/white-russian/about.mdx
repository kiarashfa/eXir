The drink descends from the Black Russian, credited to the bartender Gustave
Tops at the Hotel Metropole in Brussels around 1949, mixed for Perle Mesta,
an American socialite then serving as United States ambassador to Luxembourg
<Cite ref="spiritshunters"/>. That original was only vodka and coffee
liqueur, served over ice with nothing else added <Cite ref="spiritshunters"/>.
Cream, the addition that makes a Black Russian white, followed later, in
keeping with the taste for sweet, milky cocktails through the 1950s
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The name reaches print only some years afterward. The earliest documented use
of "white Russian" for the cocktail is a recipe run in the Oakland Tribune on
21 November 1965, calling for equal parts vodka, cream and Coffee Southern, a
coffee liqueur brand no longer made <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Neither the black
nor the white version has any real connection to Russia beyond vodka's
association with it — the name describes the base spirit, not any place the
drink was invented or served <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The drink's modern visibility owes more to cinema than to any bar. Its
prominence in the 1998 film *The Big Lebowski*, whose protagonist orders one
repeatedly and refers to it at one point as a "Caucasian," reintroduced the
cocktail to a much wider audience than the bars still pouring it had reached
on their own <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The float of cream on top, unstirred, is
the one technical detail that survives from the original Black Russian
unchanged: everything below it is built exactly as Tops built it in Brussels.
