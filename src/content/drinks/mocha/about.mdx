The name is older than the drink and refers to a place, not a flavour. Mocha
was a Red Sea port in Yemen that became Arabia's principal outlet for coffee,
handling most of the export trade between the fifteenth and seventeenth
centuries <Cite ref="smithsonian"/>. Beans that shipped through it were sold
under the port's name, and European drinkers of the period associated their
taste with a natural chocolate note, which is the reason the word attached
itself to chocolate at all rather than to any recipe <Cite ref="smithsonian"/>.

The drink now sold under that name has a separate and much later origin.
Turin had its own coffee-and-chocolate tradition first: chocolate, coffee and
cream were combined there from the seventeenth century into a drink called
*bavareisa*, which by the eighteenth century had settled into the *bicerin*,
served in a small glass so its three layers stayed visible
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The specific pairing of espresso, milk and cocoa that
the word "mocha" now names is later still — before around 1900 the word meant
only Yemeni coffee, and a recipe combining milk, coffee and cocoa under the
name "Chilled Mocha" is on record only from 1920 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The two threads are connected by little more than a coincidence of taste: a
bean from a Yemeni port that happened to read as chocolatey to the Europeans
who bought it, and a Northern Italian drink built from chocolate and coffee on
purpose, which eventually took the bean's name for a flavour it never
originally described. The espresso base keeps the drink closer to the
espresso-and-milk family it is served alongside than to the Turin original,
which is built without a shot at all.
