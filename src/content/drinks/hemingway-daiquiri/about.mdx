Constantino Ribalaigua Vert, head bartender and later owner of El Floridita
in Havana, is credited with both the standard Daiquiri served there and this
variation on it <Cite ref="npr"/>. According to an account from Hemingway's
niece Hilary, the writer tried the bar's usual Daiquiri sometime in the early
1930s, then asked for a second one with less sugar and more rum
<Cite ref="npr"/>. Ribalaigua's response, doubling the rum and dropping the
sugar, gave the drink its Cuban name: Papa for Hemingway's own nickname, and
doble for the extra spirit <Cite ref="npr"/>.

Why Hemingway asked for it that way is not settled. One account attributes it
to a fear of diabetes <Cite ref="deepsouth"/>; another names haemochromatosis,
a hereditary disorder of iron and sugar metabolism, as the reason
<Cite ref="diffords"/>. Neither source corroborates the other's diagnosis, and
the drink's history is usually told with the medical claim attached loosely
rather than as settled fact.

The grapefruit juice and maraschino liqueur that mark the recipe as served
today are a later addition. Antonio Meilan, who succeeded Ribalaigua as head
bartender at El Floridita, is credited with adding both to the plain rum,
lime and no-sugar formula, and it was under Meilan that the drink took on the
name it is now more commonly known by, the Hemingway Special
<Cite ref="diffords"/>. Ribalaigua's own Daiquiri came in several recorded
strengths and variations besides this one, of which the Papa Doble is the
one that kept a customer's name attached to it after the bar itself changed
hands <Cite ref="npr"/>.
