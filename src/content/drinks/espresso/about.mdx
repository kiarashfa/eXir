The word describes a method before it describes a drink: coffee made
specifically, on demand, rather than brewed in a pot for whoever is there. The
first patent for a machine built to do this was Angelo Moriondo's, granted in
Turin on 16 May 1884 for equipment controlling steam and water separately as
they passed through the grounds <Cite ref="wikipedia-machine"/>. Moriondo built
only a handful of machines and kept them in his own two establishments, so the
patent did not reach the market it eventually created
<Cite ref="wikipedia-machine"/>.

The machine that did was Luigi Bezzera's, patented in Milan in 1901 and bought
in 1903 by Desiderio Pavoni, who founded La Pavoni and put it into daily
production <Cite ref="pdg"/>. Bezzera's design introduced the portafilter still
in use today, and the combined machine was shown at the Milan World Fair in
1906 <Cite ref="pdg"/>. None of this equipment produced what is now considered
the drink's defining feature. Early machines ran on steam pressure alone, at
around one and a half to two bar, closer to a fast drip than a true extraction
<Cite ref="pdg"/>.

That changed after the Second World War, when Achille Gaggia replaced steam
pressure with a spring-loaded piston lever, raising the pressure through the
puck to eight or nine bar and, by accident, producing the layer of tan foam he
called *caffè crema* <Cite ref="pdg"/>. Gaggia's lever machine also fixed the
serving size at roughly the short, concentrated shot served today
<Cite ref="pdg"/>. The pump-driven machines that followed, the Faema E61 among
them, replaced the barista's arm with a motor but kept the same nine-bar
target, which is still the figure the equipment is built around
<Cite ref="wikipedia-machine"/>.

Everything else served under the name — a latte, a cappuccino, an
Americano — starts from this shot and adds to it. It is the reference
extraction the rest of the coffee list is measured against: the same beans,
pulled the same way, before milk, water or anything else changes what reaches
the cup.
