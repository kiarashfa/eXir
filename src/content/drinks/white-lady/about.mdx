Harry MacElhone created the first White Lady in 1919 at Ciro's Club in
London <Cite ref="giffard"/>, and it bears little resemblance to the drink
served under the name today: equal parts white crème de menthe, triple sec
and lemon juice, a mint-forward build rather than a gin one
<Cite ref="sipsmith"/>.

MacElhone revisited the recipe after moving to his own bar, Harry's New York
Bar in Paris, publishing a reworked version in 1929 that dropped the crème de
menthe for gin and moved from triple sec to Cointreau specifically
<Cite ref="giffard"/>. The change turned a sweet, minty drink into a dry
citrus sour built on a spirit rather than a liqueur.

Harry Craddock, running the American Bar at London's Savoy Hotel, published
his own version in *The Savoy Cocktail Book* in 1930, increasing the
proportion of gin against the liqueur and lemon <Cite ref="sipsmith"/>. That
ratio, rather than MacElhone's original mint recipe or his first gin
revision, is the one that took hold as the standard White Lady.

A later Savoy head bartender, Peter Dorelli, added egg white to his own
serve for a fuller texture, a variant still poured at some bars alongside the
version without it <Cite ref="sipsmith"/>.
