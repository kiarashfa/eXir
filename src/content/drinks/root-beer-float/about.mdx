The wider category the root beer float belongs to, the ice cream soda, is
most often credited to Robert M. Green, a Philadelphia soda-fountain
operator. By his own account, published decades later in 1910, he combined
vanilla ice cream with flavoured soda water to draw customers away from a
competing vendor at the Franklin Institute's fiftieth-anniversary
celebration in 1874 <Cite ref="wiki"/>. That version — a deliberate piece of
product development — sits at odds with the more romantic story usually
told about him, that he ran out of ice for his cream mixer on a hot day and
substituted ice cream on the spot <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Green was confident
enough of his own priority to have "Originator of the Ice Cream Soda"
engraved on his tombstone <Cite ref="wiki"/>, but he was not the only
claimant: at least three other people, including his own employee George
Guy, are separately credited with combining ice cream and soda around the
same period, with Guy's own claim placing the moment as early as 1872, two
years before Green's <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The root beer version specifically carries a separate and less documented
legend of its own. One widely repeated account credits a Colorado brewer
with dropping ice cream into root beer in 1893, inspired by the sight of
snow on a mountainside, and naming the result the Black Cow — the name the
drink still sometimes carries <Cite ref="backthen"/>. Unlike Green's account,
this version rests on retellings rather than on a contemporary source, and
it is not the story followed by more general histories of the ice cream
soda, which do not mention it at all <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The float's
invention is best treated as unsettled among several competing accounts
rather than attributable to any single one of them.
