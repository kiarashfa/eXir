Tiger nuts, small tubers rather than a true nut, arrived on the Iberian
peninsula during Muslim rule beginning in the eighth century and found
particularly favourable growing conditions in the soil around Valencia
<Cite ref="visitvalencia"/>. Written references to "llet de xufes," tiger
nut milk, appear locally by the thirteenth century, making it one of the
clearer lines back from a modern Spanish drink name to a documented
medieval one <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Valencia has never stopped growing
the crop the drink depends on: chufa now carries its own protected
designation of origin, and the region's orchards still supply the tubers
that go into both the drink sold from horchaterías and the version made at
home <Cite ref="visitvalencia"/>.

Local tradition also credits the drink's name to an encounter between King
James I of Aragon and a young Valencian woman who served it to him, with
the king supposedly exclaiming that what he was drinking was not milk but
gold. It is told as a piece of civic folklore rather than a documented
event, and no source treats it as history rather than legend
<Cite ref="visitvalencia"/>.

This is the drink the far more widely known Mexican horchata is named
after and adapted from, not the other way around: when the drink crossed
the Atlantic with Spanish colonists, tiger nuts did not grow readily
across most of Mexico, so cooks substituted rice for a similar pale,
milky result <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The two now share only a name and a
general idea of what the drink should look like; a Valencian ordering
horchata and a Mexican ordering horchata are asking for two different
plants.
