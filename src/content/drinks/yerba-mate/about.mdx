The Guaraní, spread across what is now Paraguay, northeastern Argentina,
southern Brazil and Uruguay, drank mate in pre-Columbian times, toasting
and drying the leaf of *Ilex paraguariensis* and steeping it in a gourd
sipped through a cane straw — the same basic vessel and technique still
used today <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="yerbacrew"/>. Spanish colonisers
recorded the practice from the fifteenth century onward, and it spread
through the wider Southern Cone over the following two centuries
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The plant's path to cultivation was not straightforward. Spanish
authorities banned the drink at first, suspicious of an indigenous
practice they did not control, before Jesuit missionaries reversed that
position once they saw how widely it was valued <Cite ref="yerbacrew"/>.
From the mid-seventeenth century, Jesuit missions went further and
domesticated the plant outright, establishing plantations within their
reducciones in the Argentine province of Misiones — a shift from wild
harvesting to organised agriculture that outlasted the Jesuits' own
expulsion from the region in the 1770s <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The gourd and metal straw, called the bombilla, are not incidental
tableware but the mechanism the drink depends on: the straw acts as both
sipping tube and filter, holding back the loose leaf while letting the
liquid through <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Drinking is built around sharing a
single gourd rather than individual servings — one person, the cebador,
refills and passes it round a circle, and the etiquette runs in reverse of
what a Western table would expect: a drinker who says thank you is
understood to be declining another turn, not offering thanks for the one
just finished <Cite ref="wiki"/>. What had been a rural custom moved into
cities across the twentieth century, helped in the 1960s by the
introduction, in Uruguay, of the insulated flask that let the hot water
travel with the drinker rather than staying by the kettle
<Cite ref="yerbacrew"/>.
