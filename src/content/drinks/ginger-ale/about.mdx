Ginger ale descends from a much older English tradition of home-brewed
"small beers" flavoured with ginger and other warming spices, a low-alcohol
style with roots going back centuries before it became a commercial soft
drink <Cite ref="chowhound"/>. The first commercial ginger ale is credited to
Thomas Joseph Cantrell, an Irish apothecary and surgeon working in Belfast in
the 1850s <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Cantrell's version, later called the "golden"
style, was dark, sweet and strongly spiced, made by fermentation rather than
by carbonating a syrup <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

A second, paler style overtook it within a few decades. The Canadian chemist
and pharmacist John J. McLaughlin, who had been bottling soda water since
1890, introduced a lighter "Pale Dry" ginger ale in 1904, patented in 1907
under the Canada Dry name <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Rather than fermenting a
ginger brew, McLaughlin's method flavoured carbonated water directly, which
produced a milder, less assertively spiced drink than Cantrell's original
<Cite ref="chowhound"/>. This dry style became the drink's dominant form
during American Prohibition, when its mild ginger flavour was widely used to
mask the taste of poor-quality bootleg spirits <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The two styles persist as distinct products where ginger ale is still sold
today, though the pale, dry style built on carbonating a flavoured syrup —
the version this page follows — is now by far the more common of the two
<Cite ref="chowhound"/>.
