Teh tarik grew out of the Mamak community, Malaysians descended from Indian
Muslim immigrants who settled the peninsula mostly during British colonial
rule <Cite ref="arabnews"/>. Their drink stalls date back at least to the
1870s, when vendors served spiced milk tea at the gates of rubber
plantations, and the pulling technique itself — pouring the tea back and
forth between two vessels — is traced to South Indian street cooks working
after 1850 <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The drink's present form is a postwar adaptation rather than an unbroken
recipe. After the Second World War, economic pressure pushed vendors
toward tea dust, a cheaper, lower grade of leaf that brewed noticeably
more bitter, and condensed milk was added specifically to cover that
bitterness — the combination that produced teh tarik as it is known today
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. The pull itself does double duty beyond showmanship:
tea master Senthil Kumar describes it as needing both short and long
motions to properly mix and aerate the drink, which also cools it fast
enough to drink sooner than a cup left to sit <Cite ref="arabnews"/>.

Those same Mamak vendors went on to build the roadside eateries now known
across Malaysia simply as mamak stalls, and teh tarik followed them into
that wider role <Cite ref="arabnews"/>. It is now treated as Malaysia's
national drink, made and drunk across the country's Malay, Chinese and
Indian communities alike, and it was included in Malaysia's 2024 UNESCO
intangible cultural heritage nomination <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
