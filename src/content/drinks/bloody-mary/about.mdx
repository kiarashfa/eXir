Credit for the drink is genuinely contested. Fernand Petiot, a bartender at
Harry's New York Bar in Paris, is said by his own account and his family's
records to have made a vodka-and-tomato-juice drink there from 1921
<Cite ref="wiki"/>, though a separate account concludes the comedian George
Jessel made the same combination in Palm Beach in 1927 after a heavy night
out <Cite ref="difford"/>. Petiot himself, interviewed by *The New Yorker* in
1964, allowed Jessel the claim while dismissing what it amounted to: "Jessel
said he created it, but it was really nothing but vodka and tomato juice
when I took it over" <Cite ref="wiki"/>. What both accounts agree on is that
Petiot did the later work — at the King Cole Room of New York's St. Regis
Hotel from 1934, he added salt, pepper, cayenne, Worcestershire and lemon
juice to what had been a two-ingredient drink, fixing the template still
used today <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The Red Snapper name dates to around 1935, when the hotel's owner is said to
have objected to "Bloody Mary" as too coarse for the room <Cite ref="difford"/>.
Which spirit the renamed drink actually used is itself disputed: one account
holds that it switched to gin, since vodka was still hard to find in America
at the time <Cite ref="wiki"/>, while another argues the base stayed vodka
throughout and the name change was cosmetic only <Cite ref="difford"/>.
Tabasco's own place in the recipe is dated later still, first documented in
print in 1946 and formalised in David Embury's 1952 revision of *The Fine
Art of Mixing Drinks* <Cite ref="difford"/>.

The name itself has no settled origin. Proposed sources include a Chicago
waitress named Mary, the actress Mary Pickford, a mangled pronunciation of
"Vladimir," and England's Queen Mary I, remembered for the executions of her
reign <Cite ref="wiki"/>. None is confirmed, and the drink is served under a
name nobody can trace with certainty to its first use.
