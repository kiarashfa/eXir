Peppermint is not an old species in the way many infusion plants are. It is
a natural hybrid of watermint and spearmint, and rather than being
gathered wild across centuries, it was first cultivated deliberately in
England only in the late seventeenth century <Cite ref="encyclopedia"/>.
What predates that cultivation is the mint family more broadly: dried
mint leaves have been found in Egyptian pyramids from around 1000 BC, and
both ancient Greek and Roman writers valued mint for the stomach rather
than for taste alone <Cite ref="encyclopedia"/> <Cite ref="fullleaf"/>.

Once identified as its own hybrid, peppermint moved quickly into formal
medicine. It was listed in the London Pharmacopoeia in 1721, and through
the eighteenth century it became a standard remedy across Western Europe
for nausea, indigestion and related complaints, the same uses claimed for
mint generically in earlier centuries but now attached to this specific
plant <Cite ref="encyclopedia"/>. Commercial cultivation expanded over the
same period, and it is from that point that peppermint appears reliably as
an ingredient in its own right — in teas and in distilled oil alike —
rather than as one mint among several used interchangeably
<Cite ref="fullleaf"/>.

As an infusion, peppermint tisane contains no *Camellia sinensis* and so
carries none of true tea's caffeine; the drink is built entirely on the
leaf's own volatile oils, principally menthol, released by hot water
rather than extracted the way tannins are from a tea leaf.
