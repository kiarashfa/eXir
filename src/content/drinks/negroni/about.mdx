The standard account is that Count Camillo Negroni asked a bartender at the
Caffè Casoni in Florence to strengthen his Americano with gin, at some point
around 1919 <Cite ref="diffords"/>. It is repeated everywhere, and the
documentary basis for it is thin. The fullest investigation of it is by the
Florentine bartender Luca Picchi, whose book on the subject works from civil
records rather than from anything printed at the time <Cite ref="diffords"/>, and
the earliest support anyone has produced for the story itself is a private letter
from 1920 <Cite ref="wotr"/>.

A rival claim exists and is worth stating because it is instructive rather than
because it is likely. Descendants of General Pascal Olivier de Negroni, a
Corsican-born French officer, have argued that he invented a vermouth-based
drink while stationed in Senegal in the 1850s or 1860s <Cite ref="wotr"/>. The
dates are the problem: the Campari recipe was created in 1860
<Cite ref="campari"/>, so a drink made in celebration of an 1857 marriage cannot
have contained the ingredient that defines this one.

What is documented is stranger than either. The formula appears in print in
1929, in *L'heure du Cocktail*, as equal parts gin, Campari and Italian
vermouth — under the name Campari Mixte, not Negroni. The drink is not recorded
in print under this name until 1950, and recipes titled Negroni do not appear
until 1955 <Cite ref="diffords"/>. Two decades separate the formula from the
name, which is the opposite of how the origin story is usually told.

The structure explains the survival better than the story does. It is an
Americano with the soda replaced by gin, which turns a long, low aperitivo into
a short, strong one without adding an ingredient. Equal parts makes it
memorable, and memorable is how a drink outlives the bar it was made in.

It is also weaker than its reputation. Equal thirds of gin, a twenty-five per
cent bitter and a sixteen per cent fortified wine, watered by stirring, finish
below a Manhattan and well below a Martini. What reads as strength is the
bitterness.
