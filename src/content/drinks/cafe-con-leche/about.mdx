Café con leche's origin in Spain is placed in the nineteenth century, when
milk began to be added to coffee as an everyday improvement rather than a
special preparation <Cite ref="coffee787"/>. It travelled from there through
Spanish-speaking communities, and its clearest documented outpost outside
Spain and Cuba is Tampa, where Cuban immigrants working in the cigar industry
brought it in the 1880s <Cite ref="tampahistorical"/>.

What happened in Tampa is unusually well recorded for a drink this ordinary.
Cigar factories there employed workers rolling cigars entirely by hand, in
shifts long enough that keeping them at the bench mattered to the owners as
much as keeping them awake did, and factory owners arranged for professional
cafeteros to deliver café con leche — brewed with a pinch of salt, finished
with steamed milk and sugar — directly to the rollers rather than let them
leave for a break <Cite ref="tampahistorical"/>. Beans roasted in the Cuban
style, supplied locally by mills such as Naviera Coffee, gave the coffee a
bolder flavour and a longer shelf life than a lighter roast would have kept
<Cite ref="tampahistorical"/>. The drink spread from the factory floor to
streetcar conductors and to restaurants across the city, with the Columbia
becoming particularly associated with serving it.

The Spanish and the Cuban-Tampan versions have since drifted apart in
sweetness and technique — the Spanish original left closer to plain milk and
coffee, the Cuban-influenced version built on sweetened, sometimes
sugar-whipped coffee — but both keep the same basic structure of strong
coffee lengthened with hot milk in something close to equal measure
<Cite ref="coffee787"/>.
