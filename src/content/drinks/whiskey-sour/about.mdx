The sour is a documented family rather than an inferred one. Jerry Thomas
describes it in *How to Mix Drinks* in 1862 <Cite ref="sourfamily"/>, and the
book's sours are made with brandy and with gin — spirit, lemon, sugar and water,
in that order <Cite ref="wash"/>. A whiskey one is not among them.

Whiskey arrives late and arrives already established. The earliest known mention
in print is in the *Waukesha Plain Dealer*, a Wisconsin newspaper, on 4 January
1870 <Cite ref="wswiki"/>, and it appears there as something a man simply orders,
with no explanation offered to the reader <Cite ref="wash"/>. A drink does not
reach that state in a newspaper the year it is invented, so the printed date is a
floor rather than a birthday.

A rival claim exists. A 1962 publication of the Universidad del Cuyo cites the
Iquique newspaper *El Comercio* in attributing the drink to Elliott Stubb in
1872 <Cite ref="wswiki"/>. The same claim, with the same man and the same port,
is attached elsewhere to the Pisco Sour, which is a reason to hold it loosely
rather than a reason to discount it.

The older story told about the family — sailors adding citrus to spirit against
scurvy, and the ration that became grog — is a claim about where the shape came
from, not about this drink <Cite ref="wash"/>. It is repeated widely enough to be
worth naming and rests on inference rather than on any recipe.

What is not in doubt is the proportion. Two parts spirit to three quarters each
of citrus and sugar produces a drink that is neither sweet nor sour, and the
whole family is that ratio with one slot changed. The named variants change
exactly one thing each: a white makes it a Boston Sour, a float of red wine makes
it a New York Sour, and swapping the whiskey names it after the whiskey
<Cite ref="wswiki"/>.
