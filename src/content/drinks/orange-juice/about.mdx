Oranges were eaten whole and treated as an occasional, largely seasonal
luxury in the United States through the early 20th century, rather than
juiced and drunk daily <Cite ref="juicenews"/>. That changed with a single
advertising campaign: facing a surplus of California oranges, the growers'
cooperative later known as Sunkist hired the advertiser Albert Lasker, whose
agency ran the slogan "Drink an Orange" alongside claims for the fruit's
health benefits <Cite ref="juicenews"/>. The campaign also distributed cheap
hand juicers to households, which made squeezing oranges at home practical
for the first time on a mass scale <Cite ref="juicenews"/>. Orange juice
went from a rarely consumed product to a standard part of the American
breakfast within a few decades of that campaign <Cite ref="juicenews"/>.

The juice's year-round availability came later and for a different reason.
During the Second World War, the United States government funded research
into a better-tasting vitamin C source for soldiers' rations than the
powdered lemon crystals then in use, working with Florida's citrus
scientists <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The resulting frozen concentrate was not
finished until after the war ended, but by 1949 Florida's processing plants
were producing over ten million gallons of it a year, sold as a cheap,
convenient product that only needed water added back <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Later reformulations moved back toward fresh juice rather than away from
it: a "reconstituted, ready-to-serve" style using fresher processing methods
appeared in the 1980s, followed by "not from concentrate" juice in the
1990s, closer in method to the version pressed fresh at the glass that this
page follows <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
