Powdered tea reached Japan in the late twelfth century, brought back by the
Zen monk Eisai, and the earliest preparations were dense rather than light
— closer to what is now called koicha than to the thin, frothed style more
commonly drunk today <Cite ref="japanesetea"/>. The formal split between
the two only developed later, as tea practice spread out from monasteries
into samurai households and beyond, and needed both a ceremonial centrepiece
and a lighter, more shareable everyday form <Cite ref="japanesetea"/>.

The sixteenth-century tea master Sen no Rikyū, credited with much of the
Japanese tea ceremony's present form, is recorded in period texts as
favouring usucha specifically, even though koicha occupies the ceremonial
high point of a formal gathering <Cite ref="japanesetea"/>. The likely
reason given is practical rather than aesthetic: usucha uses less tea, is
easier to drink and can be shared across a wider group, where koicha is
reserved for smaller, more formal occasions <Cite ref="japanesetea"/>.

The two are not just diluted versions of one recipe. Koicha uses roughly
double the matcha in about half the water of usucha, and it is not whisked
at all in the usual sense: the chasen kneads and folds it into a smooth,
bubble-free paste, where usucha is whisked hard in a zigzag specifically to
raise a foam <Cite ref="sugimoto"/> <Cite ref="japanesetea"/>. That foam
softens usucha's astringency; koicha is drunk without it, closer to the
tea's unmediated flavour, and is traditionally made from a single bowl
shared among the guests at a formal tea gathering rather than poured
individually <Cite ref="japanesetea"/>.
