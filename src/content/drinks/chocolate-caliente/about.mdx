Chocolate as a drink is far older in Mexico than the whisk it is now made
with. Cacao residue in Olmec earthenware has been dated to around 1750 BC,
prepared as a sour, stimulating ritual drink long before the Maya refined it
with cornmeal and chilies into a bitter, foamy beverage and the Aztecs
adopted their own cold version, valuing cacao beans highly enough to use them
as currency <Cite ref="naatik"/>. The word chocolate itself is widely traced
to the Nahuatl *xocolatl*, "bitter water" <Cite ref="naatik"/>.

The molinillo — the carved wooden whisk spun between the palms to raise a
foam — is popularly treated as a continuation of that pre-Columbian
tradition, and one account of Mexican chocolate history describes it that
way without qualification <Cite ref="naatik"/>. A closer, source-based
argument disputes this directly: pre-Columbian texts describe raising foam by
pouring chocolate repeatedly between two vessels rather than whisking it, no
molinillo appears in surviving pre-conquest writing, and the tool is absent
even from the first Nahuatl-Spanish dictionary, compiled in 1571 — evidence
that points to the molinillo as a Spanish-introduced tool from around the
sixteenth century rather than an Aztec one, with at least one later
engraving mistakenly placing it in a pre-conquest scene and helping cement
the myth <Cite ref="chocolateclass"/>. That reading treats the molinillo as
a genuinely mestizo object: a colonial-era tool built for an indigenous
drink, not a survival of one.

What is not disputed is what the Spanish added once chocolate reached
Europe's colonial households: cinnamon, milk and sugar, worked into a drink
that had previously run on water, cornmeal and chile <Cite ref="naatik"/>.
That combination, whisked to a foam, is the hot chocolate served in Mexico
today, and the same molinillo technique carries over into atole and
champurrado, the corn-based drinks chocolate is added to rather than
replaces.
