Coffee reached Vietnam with French colonial rule, and both the plant and the
drink kept that origin visible for the century that followed. A French
Catholic priest is credited with bringing the first arabica tree to the north
of the country in 1857 <Cite ref="barista"/> <Cite ref="heritageline"/>.
Robusta, better suited to the climate and soil of the central highlands,
followed in the early twentieth century and has dominated Vietnamese
production ever since <Cite ref="heritageline"/>.

Condensed milk entered the drink for a reason that had nothing to do with
taste at first. Fresh dairy cattle were not established in the region, and
French residents wanting milk faced the same problem colonial households
faced elsewhere in the tropics: fresh milk spoiled long before it could be
shipped in useful quantity <Cite ref="heritageline"/>. Sweetened condensed
milk, processed to keep without refrigeration, solved the logistics, and it
turned out to suit the coffee it was paired with — cutting the bitterness and
smoke of a dark Vietnamese robusta roast in a way fresh milk would not have
<Cite ref="barista"/> <Cite ref="heritageline"/>. What began as a substitute
for something unavailable became the standard, and the drink is still built
that way even where fresh milk is now easy to find.

The phin itself, the small metal filter set directly over the cup or glass,
is unrelated to that history and simply the brewing method the coffee arrived
with, unchanged by later disruptions to the industry. Vietnamese coffee
production itself was interrupted for the following decades and was rebuilt
as a state priority only after 1986 <Cite ref="barista"/>, and it is on the
back of that rebuilding that Vietnam became one of the world's largest coffee
exporters, still serving the coffee it grows over condensed milk and ice
exactly as the nineteenth century left it.
