Cold tea predates sweet tea as commonly served today by decades. American
and English cookbooks from the early nineteenth century already carried
recipes for chilled tea punches, made with green tea and heavily spiked
with alcohol, under names such as Regent's Punch <Cite ref="whatscooking"/>
<Cite ref="southernbreeze"/>. The oldest printed recipe close to the
modern, non-alcoholic drink appears in Marion Cabell Tyree's 1879
*Housekeeping in Old Virginia*, which called for green tea steeped in
boiling water, strained over ice, and sweetened with granulated sugar
<Cite ref="whatscooking"/>.

Black tea did not replace green in these recipes until around the turn of
the twentieth century, driven largely by cheaper imports from India,
Ceylon and parts of Africa displacing the Chinese green tea that had
dominated American cookbooks until then <Cite ref="whatscooking"/>. Richard
Blechynden's iced tea stand at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair is
frequently credited with inventing the drink, but the timeline does not
support that: printed recipes and restaurant menus already carried it
before the fair, and Blechynden's stand is better understood as a
popularising moment than an invention <Cite ref="whatscooking"/>.

Prohibition, from 1920 to 1933, gave the drink a further push as a
respectable substitute for the alcoholic punches it had partly descended
from, and by the 1930s sweetened iced tea had settled into the form served
today, appearing in nearly every Southern cookbook of the period
<Cite ref="southernbreeze"/>. Its association with the American South
specifically — rather than with iced tea generally, which is drunk far
more widely — is a product of that mid-century period, and it has stayed
tied to Southern identity closely enough that Georgia's state legislature
considered a bill in 2003 that would have required restaurants to serve
it <Cite ref="southernbreeze"/>.
