Donn Beach, known professionally as Don the Beachcomber, created the Zombie
at his Hollywood restaurant in late 1934 <Cite ref="wiki"/>. He guarded his
recipes with a coded system of house-labelled bottles so that even his own
bartenders could not reproduce them elsewhere, and the Zombie's exact
composition stayed a trade secret for decades <Cite ref="punch"/>. The drink
reached a wider audience at the 1939 New York World's Fair, where it was
served at a stand run by Monte Proser, who went on to claim public credit for
inventing it himself <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Don the Beachcomber's restaurants limited guests to two Zombies at a sitting,
a restriction the bar attributed to the drink's strength — three rums at
different proofs behind a mostly fruit-forward front <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The
name traded on the same idea: a guest who drank past the limit was said to
come away looking like the walking dead.

The published recipe was reconstructed only in 2005, when the bartender and
tiki historian Jeff Berry was given a notebook kept by Dick Santiago, who had
worked at Don the Beachcomber's for fifteen years, by Santiago's daughter
<Cite ref="punch"/>. Berry's research, later published in his book *Sippin'
Safari*, established that Beach changed the formula more than once between
1934 and the 1950s <Cite ref="punch"/>. The version on this page follows
Berry's reconstruction of the earliest, 1934 formula: three rums, the
grapefruit-and-cinnamon blend Beach called Don's Mix, falernum, lime,
grenadine, a dash of absinthe and a dash of Angostura <Cite ref="punch"/>.
Modern bars usually shake it and strain it over crushed ice <Cite ref="punch"/>;
this page follows the older blender method instead, the whole drink pulsed
for a few seconds with cracked ice and served in a tall tumbler
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.
