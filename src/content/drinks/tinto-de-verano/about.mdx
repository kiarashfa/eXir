The drink is usually traced to Córdoba in the 1920s, where a wine seller
named Federico Vargas is said to have cut his red wine with soda to make it
lighter for his customers, with the mixture known at first simply as "un
Vargas" <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="irishtimes"/>. Wikipedia's own account
flags the claim as under-sourced rather than settled fact, and no
independent record of Vargas or his bar has been produced beyond the story
itself <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Whatever its origin, the drink kept to one idea: young red wine and a
lemon-flavoured carbonated soda, mixed in roughly equal parts and served
over ice, with nothing else added <Cite ref="wiki"/>. That plainness is the
whole distinction from sangria, which layers in fruit, sugar and often a
spirit and is left to macerate before it is served. A tinto de verano is
built to order and drunk immediately, and in the south of Spain it is
treated as the everyday choice precisely because the wine itself is still
recognisable in the glass, where sangria's is not <Cite ref="irishtimes"/>.

Outside Spain the two are frequently conflated, in part because sangria is
the better-known export and tourist-facing venues serve it far more than
locals do; within Spain, tinto de verano is understood as the more
domestic, unfussy drink of the pair <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="irishtimes"/>.
