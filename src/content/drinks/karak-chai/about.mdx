Karak chai descends from Indian masala chai, brought to the Arabian Peninsula
by South Asian workers during the Gulf's economic boom and adapted there into
a stronger, more concentrated brew than the tea it was based on
<Cite ref="schooloftea"/>. The name is itself a fusion of two languages: *chai*
is the Hindi and Urdu word for tea, and *karak* is Arabic for strong, together
naming exactly what distinguishes the drink from a plainer cup
<Cite ref="schooloftea"/>.

The people who carried the recipe are also the people most identified with
drinking it today. Karak is sold cheaply from small stalls and
cafés — a paper cup for well under a dollar — and it remains associated with
the South Asian migrant workforce whose arrival brought it
<Cite ref="juggernaut"/>. It has become, by that account, a genuine
fixture of Emirati social life — drunk at gatherings, weddings and family
meetings — even though its ingredients and technique trace to a workforce
that arrived from outside the region rather than to a local tea tradition
<Cite ref="juggernaut"/>.

What sets the Gulf version apart from the Indian chai underneath it is the
same variable this family of drinks turns on everywhere: strength. The leaf
is boiled harder and longer, the milk and sugar are heavier, and cardamom is
boiled with the tea rather than added afterward, producing something more
concentrated than a home cup of masala chai and closer to what a small
roadside stall can sell quickly and cheaply, cup after cup, through a working
day.
