Ginger is indigenous to southern China <Cite ref="mccormick"/>, and Chinese
use of the root as a digestive remedy is reckoned in thousands of years
rather than centuries, treated in traditional medicine as a warming
ingredient for ailments brought on by cold and damp <Cite ref="mccormick"/>.
Its pairing with tea is documented specifically to the Tang dynasty, when
ginger was one of several strong flavourings — alongside onion, orange peel,
cloves and peppermint — added to counteract the bitterness of the tea of the
period <Cite ref="wiki-gingertea"/>. That places the combination of ginger
and tea leaf earlier than most of the world's now-familiar tea traditions,
though a ginger infusion made without any tea leaf at all, closer to what
this page describes, has an equally long and separate history as a plain
medicinal drink.

The root's spread out of China long predates any of its modern preparations.
Trade carried it across Asia and eventually to Europe, where it was worked
into bread and confections for its digestive effect by the medieval period
<Cite ref="mccormick"/>, and colonial trade carried it further still: Spanish
growers had established ginger plantations in Jamaica by the 1600s
<Cite ref="mccormick"/>, seeding the Caribbean ginger tradition that produces
ginger beer today.

The plain ginger infusion — root simmered in water, taken with honey or
sugar and no tea leaf at all — now has independent, parallel names across
most of the region ginger came from and beyond it: adrak ki chai in India,
made instead with milk and black tea, sits alongside standalone versions
such as Japan's *shōgayu*, Korea's *saenggang-cha*, Indonesia's *wedang jahe*
and the Philippines' *salabat* <Cite ref="wiki-gingertea"/>. None of these is
a variant of another; they are separate regional answers to the same plant,
which is also why this page describes the root simmered plain rather than
steeped alongside a tea leaf.
