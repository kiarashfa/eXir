The drink appears on a souvenir menu from the Detroit Athletic Club dated
1916, priced at thirty-five cents, which made it the club's most expensive
cocktail at the time <Cite ref="vac"/>. Who invented it there is not
recorded, and the club's own archives name no author <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Its only other documented mention for decades is a page in Ted Saucier's 1951
book of bartenders' stories, *Bottoms Up!*, which credits the vaudeville
performer Frank Fogarty with carrying the recipe from Detroit to New York
<Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="vac"/>. Saucier's account and the club's own
1916 menu do not quite agree on the timeline, and neither source establishes
that Fogarty created the drink rather than simply spreading it
<Cite ref="vac"/>.

The recipe then went unmade for roughly half a century. In 2003, Seattle
bartender Murray Stenson found Saucier's book and put the Last Word on the
menu at the Zig Zag Café <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="vac"/>. It became a
regional favourite, drew television coverage as a new Seattle drink, and
spread from there to bars across the country, becoming one of the most
widely cited rediscoveries of the 2000s cocktail revival and the direct model
for later equal-parts drinks built the same way <Cite ref="vac"/>.
