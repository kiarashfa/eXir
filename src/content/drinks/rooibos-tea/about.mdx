Rooibos, *Aspalathus linearis*, grows wild only in South Africa's Cederberg
region, and the Khoisan people who lived there are understood to have known
the plant early on, though the historical record does not clearly establish
that they brewed it as tea or used it medicinally the way it is used today
<Cite ref="klipopmekaar"/>. The first written account is European: the
naturalist Carl Thunberg documented the wild plant in the Cederberg
mountains in 1772, noting its taste and aroma, after which settlers took to
it as a cheaper local alternative to imported black tea <Cite ref="klipopmekaar"/>.

The industry now built around it is comparatively recent. Benjamin
Ginsberg, a Russian immigrant who had been trading on foot and by mule cart
through the Cederberg valleys, learned of rooibos from local harvesters and
began buying and reselling it around 1903, opening a shop in Clanwilliam
the following decade and building the trade into a real business
<Cite ref="wiki-ginsberg"/>. In 1904 he worked out the fermentation, or
"sweating," process still used to turn the green leaf a deep red-brown, and
he marketed the finished tea under the brand Eleven O'Clock, still the
oldest surviving packaged rooibos label <Cite ref="wiki-ginsberg"/>
<Cite ref="klipopmekaar"/>.

Growth outpaced supply through the 1920s because nobody could reliably
germinate the plant from seed. The problem was solved by Pieter le Fras
Nortier, a doctor and Ginsberg's business partner, who found in the 1930s
that scarifying the tough seed coat by grinding it and sowing the seed in
acidic, sandy soil made cultivation possible, turning rooibos from a wild
harvest into a farmed crop <Cite ref="klipopmekaar"/>.
