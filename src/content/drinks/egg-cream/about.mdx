Neither of the two competing origin stories fully explains the drink's name,
which is the one detail every account agrees is unresolved. One version
credits Louis Auster, a candy store owner on the Lower East Side, with
inventing the drink around 1890 and guarding his recipe closely enough that
its exact syrup was never confirmed in his lifetime <Cite ref="imbibe"/>.
The other credits the Yiddish theatre star Boris Thomashevsky, who is said to
have asked a soda fountain for a "chocolat et crème" he remembered from
Paris in the 1880s, though neither source can explain how that request
turned into the drink or the name actually used today <Cite ref="chowhound"/>.

What both accounts agree on is the ingredients, which is also the drink's
best-known joke: an egg cream contains no egg and no cream, only milk,
chocolate syrup and seltzer, mixed hard enough to raise a foamy head that
one theory holds is the real source of the name <Cite ref="chowhound"/>. The
syrup most associated with an authentic version is Fox's U-Bet, made from
1903 by the Brooklyn manufacturer Herman Fox, reportedly starting out of his
own tenement home <Cite ref="chowhound"/> — the reference bottle a recipe
calling simply for "chocolate syrup" is usually assumed against, though any
pourable chocolate syrup will make a recognisable version.

The drink's home in Jewish immigrant neighbourhoods was not incidental.
Seltzer itself arrived in the United States largely through German Jewish
bottlers working from naturally carbonated springs, and the practice
carried into New York's Jewish enclaves before the egg cream existed to use
it <Cite ref="chowhound"/>. The drink's popularity has stayed tied to that
same geography ever since, more associated with a handful of Lower East
Side and Brooklyn counters than with any national soda-fountain chain.
