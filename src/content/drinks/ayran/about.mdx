The drink is old enough that its earliest written trace only confirms it was
already familiar. Mahmud al-Kashgari's *Dīwān ul-Lughat al-Turk*, a Turkic
dictionary compiled around 1000 CE, defines ayran simply as "a drink made
out of milk," with no explanation of how to make it — evidence that the word
needed none for its intended reader <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The name itself
is thought to trace to an Old Turkic root meaning "to separate," a plausible
description of curdled milk worked apart from its whey rather than a claim
about the drink's own invention <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Its origin is explained more by necessity than by taste. Nomadic Turkic
herders in Central Asia needed to keep milk usable for longer than it would
otherwise last, and fermenting it into yoghurt slowed spoilage but left a
product too thick and sour to drink in any real quantity; diluting that
yoghurt with water and salting it produced something that kept, replaced the
minerals sweated out in a hot, dry climate, and could be drunk by the cupful
<Cite ref="jidlo"/>. The Ottoman Empire's expansion carried the same drink,
under regional names, across a belt of territory running from the Balkans
through Anatolia and the Levant into Central Asia, and it remains a fixture
in most of those places today <Cite ref="jidlo"/>.

No single modern country can claim to have originated it, whatever the
strength of its association with Turkey now <Cite ref="jidlo"/>. What has
stayed constant since the nomadic version is the proportion of idea rather
than of ingredient: yoghurt, water and salt, whisked rather than blended, and
drunk plain rather than dressed with fruit, sugar or spice.
