Matcha itself is far older than the drink built from it. Powdered tea reached
Japan from China with the Zen monk Eisai in the late twelfth century, and the
shade-growing method that produces the matcha used today developed later,
between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries around Uji, near Kyoto
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. In that tradition the powder is whisked into hot
water alone and drunk from the bowl it is made in — the preparation this
site documents separately as matcha on its own, with no milk in it at all.

The milk-lengthened version is a distinct and much more recent product of
Western café culture, built on the same whisked base but adapted to the
espresso-bar format of a shot topped with steamed milk. Its spread through
mainstream coffee chains is documented rather than informal: a large chain
added a sweetened matcha green tea latte, made with Japanese matcha and
steamed milk, to its regular menu on 4 April 2006, alongside a matcha
Frappuccino sold as a limited seasonal item that year
<Cite ref="qgazette"/>. Other major chains followed with their own versions
over the following decade and a half <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The two preparations are related only by the powder itself. A bowl of matcha
prizes the texture and bitterness that come from powder whisked directly
into water with nothing to round it out; the latte is built specifically to
soften that bitterness under milk and, in most commercial versions, added
sugar, which is a different intention rather than a lesser version of the
same drink.
