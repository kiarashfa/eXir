The name comes from the Spanish and Portuguese for "bloodletting," a
reference to the drink's red colour, and Wikipedia dates the word's use for
the drink specifically to the eighteenth century <Cite ref="wiki"/>. A
longer, looser lineage is traced further back by other accounts, to the
Greek and Roman practice of cutting wine with water and flavouring it with
whatever spice or fruit was on hand, partly because water alone was often
unsafe to drink on its own <Cite ref="vinepair"/>. Spain's own wine
tradition is dated by the same account to Phoenician vine planting around
1,100 BC, later expanded under Roman cultivation <Cite ref="vinepair"/>.
Whether the drink served today is a continuous descendant of that ancient
practice or a much later invention that merely resembles it is not settled
between the sources.

Wikipedia traces a nearer precursor to medieval León, where a wine, orange,
lemon, sugar and spice mixture nicknamed "Leonese lemonade" is identified as
an ancestor, with the cocktail in something closer to its present form
dated to the early nineteenth century and linked to sangaree, a similar
wine punch drunk in the American colonies <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Sangria reached wide American attention through Spain's pavilion at the
1964 New York World's Fair, where it was served to a wide public audience
and is credited with fixing the drink in American taste from that point on
<Cite ref="vinepair"/>. Its status is now a matter of law rather than only
custom: since 2014, European Union rules restrict the name "sangria" to
wine drinks made in Spain and Portugal, with a defined minimum citrus
content and an alcohol strength between roughly four and a half and twelve
per cent; a similar drink made anywhere else must be sold under a different
name inside the EU <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
