"Corpse reviver" was bar slang before it named any particular recipe: an 1889
slang dictionary already glossed it as simply a dram of spirits, and the term
turns up in *Punch* as early as 1861 <Cite ref="totc"/>. It described a whole
loose category of hair-of-the-dog drinks rather than one formula, and several
competing recipes carried the name through the late nineteenth century, among
them a brandy, maraschino and Boker's bitters combination published in *The
Gentleman's Table Guide* in 1871 <Cite ref="totc"/>.

The version served today is Harry Craddock's. An American bartender who
crossed to London's Savoy Hotel in 1920, Craddock published two Corpse
Revivers in *The Savoy Cocktail Book* in 1930, numbered one and two, the
second appended with the warning that four of them taken in swift succession
would unrevive the corpse again <Cite ref="diffords"/> <Cite ref="totc"/>.
Craddock's own recipe specified equal parts gin, Cointreau, Kina Lillet and
lemon juice, with a dash of absinthe <Cite ref="diffords"/>.

Kina Lillet, the quinine-forward aperitif wine Craddock named, was
discontinued and reformulated as the milder Lillet Blanc in 1986, so a Corpse
Reviver No. 2 made today from that bottle reads less bitter than the one
Craddock served. Cocchi Americano, a separate producer's quinquina, is
frequently substituted as a closer match to the original profile, and its
reappearance on the market is credited with a share of the drink's modern
revival <Cite ref="totc"/>.

Of the two Corpse Revivers Craddock published, the second is by far the more
enduring; the first, built on brandy and Calvados, is comparatively rarely
made today.
