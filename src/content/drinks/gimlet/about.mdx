Lauchlan Rose patented a way of preserving lime juice without alcohol in
1867, the same year an amended Merchant Shipping Act required British ships
to carry lime juice against scurvy <Cite ref="almanac"/>. Rose's own
advertising was pushing the cordial as a mixer with spirits by the 1870s
<Cite ref="almanac"/>, and gin, favoured by Royal Navy officers over rum, is
the pairing that stuck.

The drink's name is popularly credited to Sir Thomas Desmond Gimlette, a
Royal Navy surgeon said to have urged officers to take their lime ration
mixed with their gin. The claim is widely repeated, including on the Royal
Navy's own site <Cite ref="almanac"/>, but it does not hold up well: neither
Gimlette's 1920 obituary in *The Times* nor his *Who's Who* entry mentions
inventing a drink, and an 1892 account of gin and lime juice together calls
the combination a "strange compound" rather than an established one, which
sits badly with a cocktail already carrying an officer's name by then
<Cite ref="vademecum"/>.

The earliest written recipe under the name appears in Harry MacElhone's guide
to mixing cocktails, though sources disagree on the exact edition and give
either a two-to-one gin-to-cordial ratio or equal parts <Cite ref="almanac"/>
<Cite ref="vademecum"/>. A 1920 letter from US Admiral Albert Gleaves
describing "a new drink called a gimlet" encountered in China points toward
that same decade rather than the nineteenth-century Navy the legend usually
invokes <Cite ref="vademecum"/>.

The drink owes much of its later fame to fiction rather than the sea: Raymond
Chandler's detective Philip Marlowe insists in *The Long Goodbye* (1953) that
a real Gimlet is "half gin and half Rose's Lime Juice and nothing else"
<Cite ref="almanac"/>, a line still quoted by bartenders arguing against the
fresh-lime version served alongside it today.
