Hot spiced wine has been drunk in the Nordic countries since at least the
sixteenth century, and Glogg's name comes from the Swedish *glödgat vin*,
literally "glow-heated wine" <Cite ref="wiki"/>. One retelling credits King
Gustav I Vasa, Sweden's sixteenth-century monarch, with popularising a
version made from wine, sugar, honey and spices, and with a name that later
contracted to Glogg <Cite ref="cork"/>; this is a widely repeated story
rather than one traced to a contemporary document, and it is not addressed
at all in some general accounts of the drink's history.

The recipe in something close to its modern form — built around cinnamon,
cardamom, ginger and cloves — took shape in the nineteenth century and
became especially associated with Sweden's Romantic period, when it settled
into its role as a Christmas drink <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Serving it with
raisins and almonds spooned into the bottom of the cup is specifically
dated to the twentieth century, alongside gingerbread biscuits and saffron
buns as standard accompaniments <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

What distinguishes Glogg from its German and British relatives is the
addition of a spirit — vodka, aquavit, rum or brandy — stirred into the
wine rather than left out, a detail that separates it from the plain,
unfortified spiced wine served at German Christmas markets or in British
mulled wine <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The tradition spread beyond Sweden on this
same shared Nordic base: Finland adopted its own *glögi* from the Swedish
version toward the end of the nineteenth century, and Estonia took it up
still later, in the 1980s and 1990s <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
