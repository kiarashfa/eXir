Sahlab and salep are the same word by another route: sahlab is the Arabic
form the Turkish "salep" itself derives from, both naming the ground tuber
of wild orchids before either came to name a drink built around a
substitute for it <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The drink spread with the wider
Ottoman world, and the tuber it was named for was valued highly enough
under Ottoman rule to carry a folk reputation for fattening young women
ahead of marriage, evidence of how embedded it was in everyday and
ceremonial life rather than a marginal curiosity <Cite ref="millerstreet"/>.

What now separates most sahlab from Turkish salep is supply rather than
recipe. Genuine orchid tubers are slow to harvest by hand and increasingly
restricted, and outside Turkey's own orchid-growing regions the flour has
grown scarce enough that Levantine versions of the drink are now most often
thickened with cornflour or rice flour and flavoured with vanilla instead
of the tuber itself <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The name stayed on the drink
even as the ingredient it names became the exception rather than the rule
within it.

The result is a real divergence rather than a regional spelling of one
recipe. Turkish salep, where the tuber is still used, is finished with
cinnamon and carries a faint, musky sweetness from the orchid starch itself;
sahlab made with cornflour is a cleaner, plainer canvas, and vanilla and
chopped nuts do more of the flavouring work than any trace of the root
does. Both are covered on this site as one root drink seen from two
directions, each naming what has changed.
