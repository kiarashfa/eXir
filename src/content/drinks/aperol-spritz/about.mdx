The word spritz comes from the German *spritzen*, to splash, and names a
habit rather than a recipe: 19th-century Veneto was under Habsburg
administration, and Austrian soldiers and officials who found the local
still wines too strong for their taste asked bartenders to lighten them with
a splash of water <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That habit long predates Aperol
itself.

Aperol was introduced in 1919 by the brothers Luigi and Silvio Barbieri, from
their family distillery in Padua <Cite ref="provi"/>. Its low strength and
citrus-forward, bittersweet profile made it a natural fit for the existing
spritz format, and it gradually displaced plain water and still wine in the
glass over the following decades <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Prosecco entered
the drink around the 1970s, replacing still wine and giving the modern
version its bead <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The specific three-two-one proportion now printed on the bottle — three
parts Prosecco, two parts Aperol, one part soda — is not the original folk
recipe but a formula the Campari Group set after acquiring Aperol in 2003,
promoted as a branded campaign that helped carry the drink from a regional
Italian aperitivo into an international one, particularly in the United
States <Cite ref="provi"/>. Regional and pre-campaign versions of the same
drink use looser, closer-to-equal proportions of wine and bitter liqueur
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
