Sharbat, the wider category nimbu pani belongs to, takes its name from the
Persian word for a drink of sugar and water, itself from the Arabic *shariba*,
to drink <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The tradition is old: an 11th-century Persian
medical text, the *Canon of Medicine* by Ibn Sina, records fruit syrups
served this way, and a 12th-century reference work names several distinct
Iranian sharbat styles <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The word reached English by the
early 17th century, with Francis Bacon using it in 1626 <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
Sharbat is recorded reaching India during the Mughal period, popularised
under the emperor Babur, who had Himalayan ice imported to cool it
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Nimbu pani — literally "lemon water" — is the plain citrus branch of that
tradition: fresh lemon or lime juice, sugar and water, without the added
spice mix that distinguishes its close relative, shikanji
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. The distinction between the two names is often lost
today, but it traces to a specific older preparation, sharbat-e-shikanjavi,
made by dissolving lemon juice and sugar together and setting the mixture in
the sun for several days rather than mixing it fresh — a method the *Tribune*
traces to a description of *nalpaak* in the Mahabharata, attributed to the
legendary royal cook Nal <Cite ref="tribune"/>. Shikanji strictly names that
slow-made, spiced version; nimbu pani is the everyday drink mixed to order.

Black salt and roasted cumin, both common additions to the North Indian
glass, place nimbu pani within a broader regional habit of salting and
spicing fruit drinks for the heat rather than sweetening them alone
<Cite ref="tribune"/>.
