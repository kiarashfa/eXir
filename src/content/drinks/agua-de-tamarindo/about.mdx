Tamarind is native to Africa, not to Mexico, and its long-known name in
Arabic — *tamar hind*, "date of India" — already records one leg of a much
longer journey: from Africa the tree had reached and naturalised across
India centuries before it reached the Americas at all
<Cite ref="mexiconewsdaily"/>. Spanish traders are said to have first
encountered it during the centuries of Muslim rule on the Iberian peninsula,
and to have carried it onward once Spanish ships crossed the Pacific — the
Manila galleon route, opened in 1565, ran between Acapulco and Manila for two
and a half centuries and is one plausible corridor for the fruit's arrival in
Mexico alongside direct transport from Spain <Cite ref="mexiconewsdaily"/>.
Wikipedia states more simply that tamarind reached Mexico in the sixteenth
century and was adopted there as a flavouring for aguas frescas
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>; the two accounts agree on the century and disagree
only on which of Mexico's two colonial trade routes carried it.

However it arrived, the fruit is now assumed by many to be native, having
been folded so completely into Mexican cooking and drink that its African
origin is easy to miss <Cite ref="mexiconewsdaily"/>. Agua de tamarindo
itself belongs to the wider category of aguas frescas — water, sugar and a
fruit, seed or flower, sold from large glass dispensers alongside flavours
such as horchata and hibiscus <Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — and its place in
that lineup is a matter of the fruit finding a use rather than a dated
invention behind the drink specifically. What distinguishes tamarind's
preparation from a jamaica or a horchata is the extra step the fruit forces:
the pulp has to be soaked soft and worked through a sieve to separate it
from the fibre and seeds it is naturally packed with, before it can be
sweetened and served the same way.
