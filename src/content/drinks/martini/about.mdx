The earliest recipe printed under this name appears in Harry Johnson's 1888
*Bartender's Manual*, calling for Old Tom gin, sweet vermouth, curaçao and
bitters <Cite ref="diffords"/> — a sweeter, more heavily flavoured drink than
the one now served under the same name, and close in shape to the Martinez
already circulating under its own name a few years earlier. Its relationship
to that drink is no more settled than the rest of its history: the near-
identical Marguerite, Martine and Martinez recipes of the 1880s and 1890s read
less like one drink simplifying into another than like several bars
converging on the same gin-and-vermouth idea at once.

The move to a drier style is documented through named guides rather than a
single moment. The Marguerite of 1898 is essentially an equal-parts version
with an olive rather than a cherry, and by 1904 American bars were printing
recipes specifically labelled "Dry Martini Cocktail," built on dry rather than
sweet vermouth <Cite ref="diffords"/>. From there the published ratio kept
falling across the century: a widely cited 1922 version settles near two parts
gin to one of vermouth, the 1930s moved toward three to one, the 1940s toward
four, and by the later part of the century five or six to one was standard,
with some published versions run far drier still <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The
dry, wet and perfect versions served today trace points along that same
century-long drift rather than three unrelated recipes.

The naming is undocumented rather than settled, and the standard accounts
contradict each other. One credits a bartender at San Francisco's Occidental
Hotel serving a drink to travellers bound for the town of Martinez in the
1860s; a rival version credits a bartender in that town instead; a third
derives the name from the Martini & Rossi vermouth brand rather than from any
bartender at all <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. None of the three is documented
closer to the event than decades after it, and no source resolves which, if
any, is correct.
