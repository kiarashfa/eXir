Champurrado is, by its own definition, a variety of atole rather than a
separate invention: the same corn-masa base, with chocolate as the one
ingredient that distinguishes it <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Neither half of
that combination is new, and neither is the combination. In pre-Hispanic
Mexico cacao was roasted and ground, then thickened with a small quantity
of washed, cooked and ground maize, alongside flavourings such as vanilla,
ear flower and annatto <Cite ref="caso-barrera"/>. Corn and cacao were being
drunk together, in other words, several centuries before the drink acquired
its present name — which makes champurrado less a single invention than the
settling of a very old pairing into one fixed form.

What is specifically colonial about champurrado is the sweetness and the
dairy. The Spanish gave the name *chocolate* to the cacao paste and to the
drink made by diluting it in boiling water, taken either on its own or
stirred into the maize-flour beverage already called atole — and it is that
second preparation, cacao carried in atole rather than in water, that is
known in Mexico as champurrado <Cite ref="caso-barrera"/>. Cane sugar,
brought into the Spanish trade network from Southeast Asia, allowed sweeter
chocolate drinks than cacao alone had called for, and one early colonial
version — chocolate, vanilla and spices, served cold — sits closer to a
Spanish adaptation of a Mexican ingredient than to the hot, corn-thickened
drink served today <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The finished champurrado is a
hybrid in the same sense: an indigenous base and an indigenous technique
carried into a European idea of how a chocolate drink should be sweetened
and served.

The technique is the part that did not change. The molinillo, the carved
wooden whisk spun between the palms to raise the foam, travelled with
champurrado and with plain hot chocolate into other former Spanish colonies,
and remains the tool most associated with both <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
