An equal-parts drink of gin, creme de cacao and cream appears in print as the
Alexander Cocktail in Hugo Ensslin's 1915 guide *Recipes for Mixed Drinks*
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. An earlier drink under the same name, made instead of rye
whiskey and Benedictine, had already been printed by Jacob Grohusko in his
1910 *Jack's Manual* — an unrelated recipe that happens to share only the
name <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The switch from gin to brandy is usually credited to the bartender Harry
MacElhone, whose 1923 book *ABC of Mixing Cocktails* carries the brandy
version, still labelled simply "Alexander" rather than "Brandy Alexander"
<Cite ref="mixo"/>. A competing, earlier claim complicates that credit: the
cocktail historian Michael Crespin has traced an identical brandy, cacao and
cream recipe printed in 1913 under the name "Bach Chor Club" in a German
reference book, *Lexikon der Getränke*, a year before the earliest known gin
version reached print <Cite ref="mixo"/>. Crespin does not treat this as an
independent German invention, reasoning that the German recipe more likely
drew on an Alexander already circulating elsewhere, but the earlier date
means the print record cannot currently settle which spirit came first
<Cite ref="mixo"/>.

The name itself has no single documented source. Several explanations
circulate — a New York bartender named Troy Alexander, the Russian tsar
Alexander II, the drama critic Alexander Woollcott — but none of them is
tied to a contemporary written record, and the point is generally treated as
unresolved rather than decided <Cite ref="mixo"/>. Today's standard recipe,
including the version set by the International Bartenders Association, calls
for brandy rather than the original gin <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
