The name comes from the Clover Club, a men's club of Philadelphia
journalists and professionals chartered in 1882 and based at the
Bellevue-Stratford Hotel <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The drink is named after the
club rather than by it: no member is credited with inventing it, and the
connection is the venue where it was drunk rather than a recipe the club
itself produced.

A close relative appears in print earlier than the name does. J. A.
Grohusko's 1908 *Jack's Manual* lists a drink called the Clover Leaf, built
from gin, sugar, raspberry syrup and egg white, and describes it as popular
"in the city of brotherly love" with a soft orchid colour and a rim of white
foam <Cite ref="wiki"/>. It was garnished with a sprig of mint, which later
fell away from the recipe. The name Clover Club Cocktail is first recorded
two years later, in Raymond Sullivan's 1910 *The Barkeeper's Manual*, still
with the mint garnish attached <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Raspberry syrup and egg white are the two features that define the drink
across every version of it, the syrup giving it colour and the egg white
giving it the dense, long-lived foam that made it recognisable on sight
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. Its popularity fell away by the 1930s, a decline
attributed to drinkers' growing wariness of raw egg alongside the
complexity of getting the foam right <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
