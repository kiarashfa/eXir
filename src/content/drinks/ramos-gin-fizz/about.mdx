Henry C. Ramos created the drink at his Imperial Cabinet Saloon on Gravier
Street in New Orleans in the late 1880s, where it was first served under the
plainer name New Orleans Fizz <Cite ref="wiki"/>. What set it apart from an
ordinary gin fizz was the shake: cream, egg white and orange flower water on
top of the usual gin, citrus and sugar demanded far longer than a standard
sour to emulsify, and Ramos's own instruction called for a minimum of several
minutes and as long as twelve <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="nola"/>.

No single bartender could keep that pace and serve a room. The saloon
employed a relay of staff known as shaker boys, each taking a turn on the
tin before passing it down the line, and by the Mardi Gras season of 1915 the
Imperial Cabinet had dozens of them working at once and was still short of
demand <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="nola"/>.

Ramos kept the exact recipe to himself for over thirty years. He published it
in a New Orleans newspaper only as Prohibition approached in the late 1910s,
reasoning that a drink nobody could legally serve was no longer a recipe
worth guarding <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="nola"/>. The Roosevelt Hotel in
New Orleans later trademarked the name in 1935 and kept the drink on its own
menu from then on, including during Governor Huey Long's well-known
attachment to it <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
