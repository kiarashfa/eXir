The Bramble is credited to Dick Bradsell, a British bartender, who devised it
in 1984 while working at Fred's Club in Soho, London <Cite ref="wiki"/>. He
described the idea as coming from a memory of picking blackberries as a
child on the Isle of Wight, and wanted a cocktail that read as distinctly
British rather than borrowed from an American or continental template
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The recipe pairs gin, fresh lemon juice and sugar syrup over crushed ice with
crème de mûre — a blackberry liqueur — drizzled across the top rather than
mixed in <Cite ref="wiki"/><Cite ref="punch"/>. That last step is the
drink's identifying feature: most sours are combined to even out before they
reach the glass, and the Bramble is deliberately left uneven, the liqueur
sinking through the ice in a visible trail rather than being incorporated.
The name refers to the blackberry bush itself, tying the drink's identity
directly to Bradsell's own account of where the idea came from rather than to
any wordplay <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Unlike most of the family it belongs to, the Bramble's history carries none
of the usual disputes: it has one named creator, one documented decade of
origin, and a recipe that has changed little since. What varies between bars
is mostly the garnish and the exact ratio of syrup to lemon, not the
technique that defines it.
