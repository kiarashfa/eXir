Falooda descends from faloodeh, a Persian dessert of thin starch noodles set
in rosewater-and-lime syrup ice, still eaten in Iran today <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
The food historian K. T. Achaya traces its arrival in India to the Mughal
prince Jahangir, who is said to have encountered the dish on campaign in
Iran and brought a taste for it back with him <Cite ref="slurrp"/>. The
drink took its present form under the Mughal court and spread further
through the empire's conquests, later carried on by the Persian-influenced
successor states that governed the Deccan and Carnatic regions after Mughal
rule receded, each adding its own local variation <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The Indian version departs from its Persian ancestor in two clear ways:
milk was added where the original had none, and basil seed became the
standard addition in place of the pistachio the Persian dessert is built on
— a substitution consistent with basil growing far more readily across the
subcontinent than the pistachio does <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The change from a
frozen, noodle-set dessert to a poured, layered drink follows from the same
regional shift.

Falooda is now established well beyond India itself, eaten as a wedding and
festival drink across Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan and
Myanmar, each with variations on the same rose-syrup, vermicelli and
basil-seed base <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
