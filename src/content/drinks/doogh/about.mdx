The name outlived several changes of meaning before it settled on this drink.
It comes from the Persian *dooshidan*, to milk, and the word itself appears in
Avestan scripture as another term for milk or for what milking produces
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. It appears again, centuries later, in Persian
literature from the medieval period, and the physician Avicenna used it in
the *Canon of Medicine* to denote milk in a medical context
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — evidence of a long, continuous presence in the
language rather than of one invention at one date.

Traditionally it was made by churning yoghurt with whole milk in a skin bag,
a method that both thinned the yoghurt and worked air into it, and modern
versions still aim at the same texture using water, whey or seltzer in place
of the bag <Cite ref="tastecooking"/>. Iran's own food standards describe it
formally as a drink made by diluting fermented yoghurt, or buttermilk before
it ferments, to a standardised proportion of milk solids — a definition that
treats doogh as a controlled dairy product as much as a folk drink
<Cite ref="tastecooking"/>.

Two features set it apart from its regional relatives. It is usually
carbonated, historically by leaving the diluted yoghurt at room temperature
for a day or two to ferment lightly on its own, and it is most often finished
with mint alongside other herbs such as oregano rather than left plain
<Cite ref="tastecooking"/>. Both are matters of degree rather than of kind —
ayran, Turkey's version of the same idea, is typically still and salted only
— but they are consistent enough across recipes to count as what actually
distinguishes doogh from the drink it most resembles.
