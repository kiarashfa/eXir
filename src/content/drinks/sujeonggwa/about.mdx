The earliest clear description of the drink appears in *Dongguksesigi*, an
1849 record of Korean seasonal customs by the scholar Hong Seok-mo, which
describes a dried persimmon brew with ginger and pine nuts added
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. A later text, the 1921 *Haedongjukji*, traces the
drink further back still, to palace women of the Goryeo era preparing a
version on New Year's Day under a different name, *baekjeho*, meaning
roughly "white milky beverage," a reference to the pale look of
sugar-coated persimmons rather than to the reddish drink most people know
today <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Cinnamon, the ingredient now most associated
with sujeonggwa, is a later addition still: it enters the written recipe
only with a text called *The Recipes of Joseon*, alongside a shift from
honey to sugar as the drink's sweetener <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Because cinnamon and ginger were both costly imported spices, the drink is
generally understood to have been a palace and upper-household luxury
through the Joseon dynasty rather than an everyday drink, only spreading
more widely as spices became cheaper <Cite ref="tastingtable"/>. It was
served at the close of elaborate court meals alongside sikhye, the sweet
rice punch, in a pairing that still holds today: sikhye for a milder,
starch-sweetened finish and sujeonggwa for a sharper, spiced one
<Cite ref="tastingtable"/>. That association with marking the end of a
significant meal is also why the drink remains tied to Korea's major
holidays, served at New Year and at Chuseok rather than as an everyday
refreshment <Cite ref="tastingtable"/>.
