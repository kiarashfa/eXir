James Pimm ran an oyster bar in the City of London in the 1820s and served
his customers a gin-based tonic of herbs, spices and fruit alongside their
oysters, intended to aid digestion and kept deliberately low in alcohol
<Cite ref="revelry"/>. It was served in a small tankard called a "No. 1
Cup," and that vessel gave the drink its name once Pimm's began bottling
and selling the recipe commercially, trademarking it in 1912
<Cite ref="wiki-cup"/>. Later versions built on other spirits — Scotch,
brandy, rum, vodka and, briefly, tequila — followed under the No. 2 through
No. 6 designations, though only a handful are still made today
<Cite ref="revelry"/>.

The drink itself, rather than the bottled base alone, took shape as Pimm's
No.1 mixed with lemonade and dressed with fresh fruit and cucumber, served
long over ice <Cite ref="wiki-cup"/>. It reached the United States by the
late 1940s, most likely carried over by a British expatriate, and took hold
particularly in New Orleans, where Napoleon House in the French Quarter
built its own reputation partly on serving it through the city's summers
<Cite ref="revelry"/> <Cite ref="wiki-cup"/>.

In Britain the Pimm's Cup stayed tied to a specific season rather than
spreading evenly through the year, and it is now the closest thing the
English summer calendar has to an official drink, poured at Wimbledon, the
Chelsea Flower Show and Royal Ascot alike <Cite ref="wiki-cup"/>. The
garnish is treated as part of the recipe rather than an afterthought: what
goes into the glass beyond cucumber and mint varies by household and by
bar, and the drink has never settled on one fixed fruit bill the way a
cocktail with a stricter formula would have <Cite ref="revelry"/>.
