Punch as a category predates rum entirely. It is recorded among British East
India Company traders from the early 1600s, made first with arrack, a palm
or rice spirit, rather than any Caribbean spirit; the earliest written
reference identified is a 1632 letter from an East India Company employee
in India <Cite ref="difford"/>. Sailors are credited with the idea itself: a
shelf-stable stand-in for wine, built from whatever spirit was on hand,
citrus for acidity and sugar to round it off <Cite ref="tasting"/>.

The drink's name has a disputed origin. It is popularly traced to the
Sanskrit or Hindi word for "five," describing five standard ingredients —
spirit, citrus, sugar, water and spice — but drinks historian David
Wondrich argues instead for a source in "puncheon," the large barrel rum
itself was shipped in, and treats the five-ingredient story as folk
etymology rather than settled fact <Cite ref="difford"/>.

Rum only entered the drink once British colonial trade expanded into
sugar-producing Caribbean territory and the Royal Navy began issuing rum
rations to sailors, who carried rum, sugar and lime aboard specifically to
make punch <Cite ref="difford"/>. The formula that resulted is preserved in
an old rhyme — one of sour, two of sweet, three of strong, four of weak —
that has stayed consistent enough across generations of Caribbean bars that
modern recipes still cite it directly by ratio <Cite ref="tasting"/>. The
category's popularity in Britain itself later faded through the Victorian
era, even as the Caribbean version it left behind persisted <Cite ref="tasting"/>.
