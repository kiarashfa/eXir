Root beer descends from colonial-era American small beers brewed from
sassafras and other roots, with written recipes documented from the 1830s
and sale from confectionery stores recorded by the following decade
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. The drink was originally taken hot, for its supposed
medicinal value, before it was combined with carbonated soda water in the
1850s <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The pharmacist Charles Elmer Hires developed the
sassafras-based formula that made it a commercial product, launching it
publicly at Philadelphia's 1876 Centennial Exposition; despite being a
teetotaler who reportedly preferred the name "root tea," Hires adopted "root
beer" instead, on the reasoning that it would sell better to the
Pennsylvania coal miners he was marketing it to <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The recipe changed for a regulatory rather than a commercial reason.
Safrole, the aromatic compound that gives raw sassafras bark its
characteristic flavour, was identified through animal studies as a cause of
liver damage and cancer, and food and drug regulators in the United States
prohibited it as a food additive as a result <Cite ref="medicinenet"/>. Every
commercial root beer sold since has had to be made without whole sassafras
bark or oil, using either a specially processed safrole-free sassafras
extract or a synthetic flavouring built to approximate it, generally
supported by other root and spice flavours such as wintergreen, vanilla and
sarsaparilla <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The syrup-and-soda-water method used today,
including on this page, is a direct continuation of that reformulation
rather than of the drink's original fermented recipe.
