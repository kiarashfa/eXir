Coffee, not tea, was the drink historically associated with Turkey, and
tea only became the everyday choice once coffee grew scarce and expensive
in the disrupted trade of the First World War <Cite ref="saki"/>. Tea had
already been trialled experimentally in Rize province, on the Black Sea
coast, from 1912, but it took decades for cultivation there to produce a
meaningful crop; sizable harvests are only recorded from around 1945, with
the first local processing factory opening in 1947 <Cite ref="wiki-rize"/>.
Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's government backed the region's tea industry
through the 1930s as part of a wider push toward domestic agricultural
self-sufficiency, and Rize's climate — heavy rainfall and fertile soil
between the Pontic Mountains and the Black Sea — turned out to suit tea
particularly well <Cite ref="wiki-rize"/> <Cite ref="saki"/>.

The double kettle brewing method predates Turkey's own tea industry. The
çaydanlık, a smaller pot stacked on a larger one, is generally traced to
the Russian army in the early nineteenth century and was adopted into
Turkish tea culture well before Rize was producing tea of its own
<Cite ref="saki"/>. The lower kettle keeps a supply of plain boiling water
going; the upper pot holds a small volume of tea brewed deliberately
stronger than anyone would drink it undiluted. Heat from below rather than
direct contact with a flame keeps the leaf from scorching, and the two-pot
design lets a host serve guests at different strengths from the same brew,
simply by varying how much hot water goes in at the glass
<Cite ref="saki"/>.
