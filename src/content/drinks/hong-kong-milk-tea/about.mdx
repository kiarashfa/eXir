British afternoon tea reached Hong Kong under colonial administration but not
its ordinary population: for decades it was served almost exclusively in
hotels and high-end Western restaurants, out of reach of most residents
<Cite ref="scmp"/>. It was street stalls and cheap cafés — first the *bing
sutt*, later the *cha chaan teng* — that made a version of it affordable,
substituting evaporated milk for fresh and brewing a stronger, cheaper leaf
blend that stretched further and still stood up to milk <Cite ref="wiki-hkmt"/>.
That substitution was practical rather than aesthetic: evaporated milk kept
without refrigeration, cost less, and did not dilute the tea the way fresh
milk would <Cite ref="scmp"/>. In the 1940s the drink was brewed hard and sold
to labourers as a caffeine source; by the 1960s, as it spread through the
cha chaan teng trade, a milder version had developed for office workers
<Cite ref="wiki-hkmt"/>.

The drink's Cantonese nickname, "silk-stocking milk tea," describes the
strainer rather than the tea: the leaf is steeped and repeatedly poured
through a fine cotton bag, which after prolonged use takes on the tea's
colour and a shape said to resemble a stained silk stocking
<Cite ref="wiki-hkmt"/>. The Lan Fong Yuen café's claim to have invented this
specific straining style in 1952 is widely, though not universally, accepted
<Cite ref="wiki-hkmt"/>.

The technique is now recognised as intangible cultural heritage in Hong Kong,
and the repeated pulling through cloth remains the part hardest to reproduce
outside a professional kitchen — commentators note that a convincingly
smooth cup is difficult to make well at home <Cite ref="scmp"/>, which is
part of why the drink is still ordered out rather than made in.
