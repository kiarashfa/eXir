The drink's Creole name means "small punch," a reference to its origin
among sugarcane field workers on Martinique and Guadeloupe, who are
described as drinking it for a quick lift of sugar and alcohol during long
days of labour rather than as a leisure drink <Cite ref="punch"/>. It
predates ice being widely available in the region, which is why the classic
build has never included any <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The rhum it is built on is specific to the style. Rhum agricole is distilled
from fresh-pressed cane juice rather than the molasses most rum elsewhere is
made from, and it is most closely associated with Martinique, where it
carries its own appellation controls <Cite ref="punch"/>.

The drink's defining custom is that it is not made for the drinker but by
them. The bartender or host sets out the rum, the cane syrup, the lime and
the glass and leaves each person to build their own to taste, a habit summed
up in the island phrase "chacun prépare sa propre mort" — "each prepares
their own death" <Cite ref="wiki"/>. That same self-service tradition is
recorded independently in both regional and outside accounts of the drink,
which agree that no fixed ratio governs it the way one governs most cocktails
<Cite ref="punch"/>.
