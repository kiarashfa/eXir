Where the culture itself comes from is genuinely unsettled. One early
theory, put forward by the microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck in 1889, held
that the grains descended from the "ginger beer plant" supposedly carried
home by British soldiers returning from the Crimean War of the 1850s. The
mycologist Harry Marshall Ward rejected that account in 1892 as
unsubstantiated, writing that the culture's real origin was a mystery
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The best-documented early sighting of what is now
called water kefir instead comes from Mexico: in 1899 the researcher Lutz
reported grains, which he called tibi, growing on the pads of an Opuntia
cactus and capable of being revived in sugar water, a finding that gives the
culture its still-current Mexican name, tibicos <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The drink has carried a scatter of other names in the years since, among
them Japanese water crystals, California bees and the ginger beer plant
Ward was arguing against, reflecting how widely and informally the culture
travelled once it left any one place, passed jar to jar between households
rather than sold or documented commercially <Cite ref="wikipedia"/><Cite ref="cfh"/>.

It is easy to confuse with milk kefir because both are jar ferments kept
going by straining out and reusing a live culture, and both carry the word
"kefir." They are not the same organism, though: water kefir's grains are
built around a different community of yeast and bacteria suited to sugar
water rather than milk, and swapping one culture into the other's liquid
does not reliably work. In Mexico, tibicos are also used as the starter for
tepache, fermenting sweetened pineapple rather than plain sugar water, which
is a second, quite different drink built on the same culture.
