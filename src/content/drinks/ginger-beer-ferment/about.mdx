Brewed ginger beer is generally traced to Yorkshire in the mid-eighteenth
century, part of a much older English tradition of home brewing that
already used ginger in various drinks <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Jamaican
ginger, exported in vast quantities once England controlled the island
from 1655, made the spice cheap and reliable enough in Britain to become a
drink ingredient in its own right rather than only a costly seasoning
<Cite ref="msu"/>.

The starter culture responsible for the ferment, popularly called the
"ginger beer plant," was for a long time exactly that to the people who
kept it: a gelatinous, translucent lump passed from household to household
and treated as a single living thing. The botanist Harry Marshall Ward
received samples in 1887 and, after several years studying them, published
a description in 1892 showing it was not a plant at all but a symbiotic
culture of a yeast and a bacterium growing together, work that also
resolved competing folk theories about where the culture had come from
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Traditional ginger beer carried real alcohol,
reportedly as much as eleven percent, until the British Parliament imposed
a tax in 1855 on drinks above two percent, after which brewers deliberately
shortened fermentation to stay under the new threshold <Cite ref="wikipedia"/><Cite ref="msu"/>.

English ginger beer travelled well in the stoneware bottles it was sold in,
and export to North America from the 1790s onward built a market there
that outlasted the drink's popularity at home <Cite ref="msu"/>. American
Prohibition then pushed drinkers toward ginger ale and other soft drinks
instead, and commercial ginger beer increasingly moved to plain yeast
fermentation or to no fermentation at all, carbonated and flavoured rather
than brewed <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The version made from a home-grown
ginger bug, as on this page, is the older method rather than a modern
invention returning to it.
