Kombucha's own early history is genuinely thin, and the version most often
repeated is not well supported. A popular account traces it to China's Qin
dynasty around 220 BC, sometimes tied directly to the emperor Qin Shi Huang,
and the same story is widely repeated in marketing copy for the drink
<Cite ref="pmc"/>. It does not hold up: no contemporary record supports it,
and stories linking the drink to Korea or Japan before the twentieth century
are similarly unfounded, both flagged as promotional myth rather than
documented history <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What can be said with more
confidence is that a fermented tea of this kind was known in China's Bohai
Sea region, where some practitioners of traditional medicine used it for
stomach and lung complaints, without ever becoming a widely known drink
across the country <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Its clearer documented path runs through Russia, where it appears in print
by 1852 and became known there as "tea kvass," and from there into Eastern
and Central Europe and Germany, largely as a homemade product rather than a
commercial one <Cite ref="pmc"/><Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. It reached the
United States only in the late twentieth century, and its American uptake
was tangled up with the AIDS epidemic of the 1980s and 1990s, when some
patients took it up on unproven health claims; that momentum stalled after
a 1995 report tied several cases of metabolic acidosis to home-fermented
kombucha <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Its wider popularity today, built on
commercial bottling rather than the kitchen jar, is a recent development,
and by some estimates the drink is now better known in the West than in the
country most often credited as its source <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The culture responsible for all of this, a rubbery mat commonly called a
SCOBY, is not a single organism but a working community of yeasts and
acid-producing bacteria living together in a cellulose matrix, and it is
this community, not any one microbe, that turns sweet tea sour and lightly
carbonated over the course of a week <Cite ref="pmc"/>.
