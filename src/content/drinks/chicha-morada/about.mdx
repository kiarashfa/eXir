Something close to chicha morada was already being drunk in the Andes before
the Inca Empire existed, and colonial chroniclers recorded it being used
medicinally, in religious ritual, and as an offering to the dead — evidence
of a drink woven into Andean life well beyond refreshment
<Cite ref="mashed"/>. No source names an inventor, and none claims one exists;
the drink's age is documented through what colonial writers described
rather than through any single origin.

That older drink was, by most accounts, fermented rather than boiled.
Chicha in its broader sense covers a family of corn beverages across the
Andes, and the traditional method for activating fermentation involved
chewing the corn, since enzymes in human saliva convert its starches into
fermentable sugars <Cite ref="mashed"/>. Chicha morada specifically is the
branch of that family that colonial chroniclers describe being made by
boiling instead of fermenting, which is what makes it — unlike chicha in
general — reliably non-alcoholic <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Neither source
gives an exact date for that shift, only that it had already taken place by
the time it was being written about.

The written record for the drink much resembling today's version becomes
clearer in the nineteenth century, with detailed descriptions appearing in
the writing of the French author Camille Pradier-Fodéré in the 1870s and
earlier references in the work of Juan de Arona and Carlos Prince
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The method those sources describe — purple corn
boiled with pineapple peel, quince, cinnamon and cloves, then strained,
cooled and sweetened — is essentially the one still followed, purple corn's
colour and mild sweetness serving as the base for whatever fruit and spice a
household has on hand <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
