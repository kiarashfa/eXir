The name is French for "old square" and refers to the French Quarter, the
neighbourhood the Hotel Monteleone sits in rather than to any person
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The hotel's own account credits its head bartender,
Walter Bergeron, with creating the drink at its Carousel Bar in 1938
<Cite ref="monteleone"/>. That credit is repeated widely and has never been
supported by anything contemporary, which is worth stating plainly rather than
passing over: the date and the hotel are well attested, and the attribution to
Bergeron rests more thinly than either <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The recipe is built from two older drinks rather than from a fresh idea.
Rye whiskey and a pair of New Orleans bitters, Peychaud's and Angostura,
place it in the same lineage as the Sazerac, while cognac and sweet vermouth
in close to equal measure recall a Manhattan built with brandy instead of a
single whiskey. Bénédictine, a French herbal liqueur, is the one ingredient
that belongs to neither ancestor.

However it was arrived at, the combination sits later than both of the drinks
it draws from. The Sazerac's absinthe-and-rye build was established in New
Orleans decades earlier, and the Manhattan's whiskey-and-vermouth shape was
already standard nationally by the 1880s. The Vieux Carré is a hotel bar's
1930s answer to what happens when both are poured into the same glass rather
than a new discovery, and the four spirituous ingredients in close to equal
parts are what keep it from reading as simply a heavier Manhattan or a softer
Sazerac.
