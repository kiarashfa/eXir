The name states exactly what the drink does. *Cortado* is the past participle
of the Spanish verb *cortar*, "to cut", used here in the sense of diluting
rather than slicing <Cite ref="wikipedia"/> — the milk cuts the espresso's
sharpness without lengthening the drink the way a larger pour would. Accounts
of its origin place it in Spain's Basque region in the early twentieth
century, where it was known before the name settled as *café con leche
pequeño*, "small coffee with milk" <Cite ref="coffee787"/>.

The drink answers a specific complaint rather than reaching for a new
technique: straight espresso reads as acidic and sharp to a milk-drinking
palate, and a small amount of warmed milk — steamed but not aerated into a
real foam — softens both without diluting the coffee toward a latte's larger
proportions <Cite ref="coffee787"/> <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That distinction
from a caffè latte is the whole point of the drink: a cortado is built to a
size that only ever holds an equal-parts ratio, not a smaller latte poured
into a smaller glass.

The name and the drink both travelled to Cuba, where the same technique is
called a cortadito and takes a different form — a standard shot of espresso
finished with sweetened condensed milk rather than fresh, heated milk, a
substitution that reflects what was reliably available on the island rather
than a stylistic choice <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The two are close enough in
name and idea to be mistaken for each other, but the milk they are built on
is not interchangeable.
