The name identifies the drinker rather than the drink: workers in the British
building trade are conventionally understood to get through a great many cups
of tea across a working day, and the strong, dark-beige brew they favour
picked up their trade's name for it rather than a name of its own
<Cite ref="wiki-builders"/>. It is also known as a builder's brew or a
gaffer's tea, and the terms are used across both Great Britain and Ireland
<Cite ref="wiki-builders"/>.

What identifies the drink itself is the method as much as the trade: a
teabag steeped directly in a mug rather than a pot, brewed hard enough to
stand up to milk without turning pale, and finished with sugar — commonly a
teaspoon or two of it — stirred in either before or after the milk
<Cite ref="wiki-builders"/>. None of that is unusual English tea practice on
its own. What sets a builder's tea apart from an ordinary cup is degree: a
stronger dose, a longer steep, and a settled expectation of milk and sugar
rather than a matter of the drinker's taste, so that a weak or unsweetened cup
served under the name would be recognised as a contradiction rather than a
variant.

No exact date attaches to the term's first use, but it rose to wider public
recognition in the 1970s, in an account that ties the drink to construction
and manufacturing workers who wanted something quick to make and strong
enough to keep them going through a shift — commonly built on a darker,
more robust blend than a milder afternoon tea would use
<Cite ref="tasting-table"/>. That account and the trade-name explanation agree
on the essentials: a working drink defined by strength and speed rather than
by any particular leaf, and a name that has outlasted any claim on who
actually started making it that way.
