Rice wine brewing in Korea is traced back to the Three Kingdoms period, and
during the Goryeo dynasty a version was known as *ihwa-ju*, "pear blossom
wine," for the time of year it was traditionally made <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
The name makgeolli itself is far younger: it first appears in an encyclopedia
believed to date to the nineteenth century <Cite ref="koreanprep"/>. What has
stayed constant across that span is the method, using rice, water and nuruk,
a dried cake of grain carrying wild mould, yeast and bacteria together, which
both breaks the rice's starch down to sugar and ferments it to alcohol in the
same vessel <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Cloudy, low in alcohol next to distilled
soju, and cheap to make at home, it became known as a farmer's drink,
brewed in the countryside rather than bought <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

That home-brewing tradition was deliberately broken twice in the twentieth
century. Under Japanese colonial rule, a licensing and tax system was
imposed on home production, and by 1934 brewing makgeolli at home had been
outlawed outright <Cite ref="wikipedia"/><Cite ref="koreanprep"/>. Decades
later, in 1965, the South Korean government banned brewing with rice again,
this time over rice shortages, and producers switched to wheat and barley
for a generation <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Rice brewing was permitted again
around 1990, though ordinary home brewing did not become fully legal again
until years after that <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The drink's recent revival among younger, urban drinkers has come largely
through small breweries returning to traditional nuruk rather than the
laboratory yeast and additives that filled the gap during the ban years, a
shift Boksoondoga, a brewery founded in 2009 on a family recipe, is often
credited with helping to popularise <Cite ref="koreanprep"/>. By the late
2010s South Korea counted several hundred small-scale makgeolli producers,
a scale of production the drink had not seen since before the colonial ban
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
