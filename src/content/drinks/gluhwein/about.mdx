Heated, spiced wine is recorded as far back as Roman antiquity: the
playwright Plautus refers to it in his second-century-BC comedy *Curculio*,
and Roman legions are credited with carrying both viticulture and the
practice of heating wine north along the Rhine and Danube as they expanded
into Europe <Cite ref="wiki"/>. A named Roman recipe for spiced wine,
*conditum paradoxum*, appears in the cookbook attributed to Apicius, built
from wine, honey and spices including pepper, bay leaf and saffron
<Cite ref="crush"/>.

The name Gluhwein itself is German for "glow-wine," and the oldest surviving
vessel made specifically for it, a tankard dated to around 1420, belonged to
Count John IV of Katzenelnbogen <Cite ref="wiki"/>. One account of the name
ties it to a specific heating method: an iron rod heated in a fire until it
glowed, then plunged directly into a cup of wine, which is said to have
briefly glowed at the point of contact <Cite ref="crush"/>. A separate
account instead traces "glowing wine" to a sixteenth-century Swedish
coinage, *glödgad vin*, which is also the root given for the Swedish Glogg,
rather than to any German source <Cite ref="crush"/>; the two explanations
are not reconciled between the sources consulted here.

Gluhwein's specific association with Christmas markets is a later
development. Vienna's Christmas market dates to 1298, and Gluhwein is
described as an established feature of German-speaking Christmas markets by
the seventeenth century, sold from stalls to marketgoers against the cold
<Cite ref="crush"/>. It remains the standard hot drink at Christmas markets
from Munich to Vienna today, and in Alsace it is said to be the only
alcoholic drink sold at the market at all <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Unlike its
Nordic counterpart, it is not built with an added spirit.
