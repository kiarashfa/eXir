The drink descends from posset, a hot milk-and-alcohol drink curdled with
wine or ale and known in England since the Middle Ages; by the 13th century
monks were already recorded drinking a version made with eggs and figs
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. Egg-enriched posset later became fashionable enough among
the English aristocracy to serve as a display of wealth, since the sherry
that spiked it was imported and expensive <Cite ref="nlm"/>.

The drink took its familiar form in Britain's American colonies during the
1700s. Brandy and wine both carried heavy import taxes there, while rum
shipped cheaply from the Caribbean did not, and colonial farms kept milk and
eggs in ready supply; the combination made a rum-spiked version of the old
posset far more affordable in the colonies than the sherry-based drink had
ever been in England <Cite ref="wiki"/>. George Washington is recorded as
having served visitors a version of his own, made with rye whiskey, rum and
sherry together <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Regional preference later split the
American drink by spirit: rum stayed the base in the north while whiskey or
bourbon became more common further south <Cite ref="nlm"/>.

The name's origin has no settled account. Proposed explanations tie "nog" to
a strong beer once brewed in East Anglia, to "noggin," a small wooden mug
used to serve it, or to a blend of colonial slang for rum with that same
mug, though no version of the story is confirmed <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

Raw egg in a drink meant to be aged rather than served immediately is a real
food-safety question rather than a folk detail. Modern institutional
guidance recommends pasteurised eggs for any eggnog served fresh
<Cite ref="nlm"/>, while separate analysis of the aged style has found that
a mixture alcoholic enough and rested cold for roughly three weeks
effectively sterilises itself even when made with contaminated eggs
<Cite ref="wiki"/> — the basis for treating the long-rested version and the
cooked custard as two distinct ways of making the same drink safe, rather
than one being a shortcut of the other.
