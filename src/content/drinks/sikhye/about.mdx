Sikhye was served to Korean royalty after meals, valued as a way to settle
the stomach once the food itself was cleared <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. No
sugar is added to the traditional version at all: the sweetness comes
entirely from barley malt's own enzymes breaking the rice's starch down as
the two steep together in warm water, and only once that conversion is
done is the liquid strained, boiled and sweetened further, if at all
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The method survives essentially unchanged from
that palace kitchen into home kitchens today, carried forward mostly by
grandparents making it for a family gathering rather than by any written
recipe tradition <Cite ref="kocis"/>.

That domestic, generational transmission is what defines the drink's
present-day standing more than any specific historical date does. It is
strongly associated with Korea's major holidays, Seollal and Chuseok, where
it is served after a heavy meal, and it appears at contemporary weddings
in punch bowls finished with honey and pine nuts, a modern echo of a drink
that began as something made for a special occasion rather than an
everyday one <Cite ref="kocis"/>. Regional versions vary the ingredients
considerably: an Andong version adds radish, carrot and red pepper powder
for a savoury, faintly spicy variant, and a pumpkin version substitutes
that vegetable for much of the rice, though the same malt-and-rice
principle underlies all of them <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
