Before tea reached most of India as a drink, kadha already existed there: an
Ayurvedic decoction of spices such as ginger, cardamom and pepper, boiled in
water for medicinal use rather than refreshment <Cite ref="teatrade"/>.
Black tea itself arrived as a colonial export crop. The British East India
Company began planting Assamese tea in the 1830s specifically to break a
Chinese near-monopoly on supply, and by 1900 Indian and Ceylonese tea had
displaced most Chinese tea in Britain's own market — but almost none of it
was drunk in India, where it was grown, packed and shipped straight to
London <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="teatrade"/>.

That changed once the Indian Tea Association, facing an oversupply of tea
it could no longer sell abroad, began a domestic marketing campaign in the
early twentieth century, subsidising tea breaks in factories and mills and
tea stalls along the expanding railway network <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The
Association pushed the plain English method of steeping tea in water alone;
independent chaiwalas at those same stalls did the opposite, stretching a
smaller dose of cheap tea dust with milk, sugar and the older kadha spice
mix, both to cut cost and to suit a market already used to spiced drinks
<Cite ref="teatrade"/>. The Association initially objected to the practice
as wasteful of the tea it was trying to promote <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The version that won out was the chaiwalas', not the Association's, and it
spread through homes, markets and railway platforms as an everyday drink
rather than a colonial import <Cite ref="wiki"/>. What counts as the spice
blend is not fixed: cardamom, ginger and cinnamon are close to universal,
and cloves, black pepper, star anise or fennel appear depending on region
and household <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
