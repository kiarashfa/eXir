The recipe's first known appearance in print is Harry Craddock's 1930 *The
Savoy Cocktail Book* <Cite ref="punch"/>, and it is generally described as a
London creation of that period rather than an import <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Its
name and the equal-parts formula are tied to *Blood and Sand*, a 1922 silent
film starring Rudolph Valentino as a bullfighter, itself adapted from a 1908
Spanish novel <Cite ref="wiki"/>. No source names the drink's actual
inventor; the connection to the film is treated as settled, the person who
made it is not.

The two title ingredients are read as the film's own imagery: the cherry
liqueur — Cherry Heering in the original formula — standing for the blood of
the bullring, and the orange juice for its sand <Cite ref="punch"/>. Some
retellings specify blood orange for the juice on the strength of that same
metaphor, but the earliest published recipe calls for orange juice without
qualification, and no source traces the blood-orange detail back to
Craddock's own text.

The drink survives in the modern cocktail canon on the strength of its
history rather than universal enthusiasm for how it tastes: contemporary
bartenders quoted discussing it single out its balance as fragile, with the
orange juice's volume easily overwhelming the other three equal parts and
leaving a drink that reads as little more than spiked juice unless each line
is measured exactly <Cite ref="punch"/>.
