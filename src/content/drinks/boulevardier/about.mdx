The drink is credited to Erskine Gwynne, an American writer living in 1920s
Paris and a co-founder of a magazine named for the well-dressed men-about-town
the word describes <Cite ref="diffords"/>. The recipe appears in Harry
MacElhone's 1927 *Barflies and Cocktails*, inside an essay on the bar culture
of the city's American expatriates, and names Gwynne as its author
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The original proportions are equal parts bourbon,
Campari and Italian vermouth <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Almost nothing is recorded about the drink between that appearance and its
rediscovery during the cocktail revival of the 2000s — a gap of roughly eighty
years with no documented reference in between <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Ted
Haigh's 2007 republication of the recipe adjusted the ratio to one and a half
parts bourbon against one part each of Campari and vermouth, and it is that
heavier-on-the-whiskey version, rather than Gwynne's original equal thirds,
that most published recipes and the modern trade now call a Boulevardier
<Cite ref="punch"/>.

The shape is close enough to its gin-based relative that most modern accounts
describe it outright as a whiskey version of that drink rather than as a
separate invention <Cite ref="punch"/>. What changes is more than the base
spirit: with a rounder, sweeter spirit standing where the gin's botanicals
were, the Campari reads as less sharply bitter and the whole drink sits
fuller and softer on the palate, even with the Campari and vermouth carried
over unchanged. Both drinks are stirred rather than shaken for the same
reason — everything in the glass is clear and spirituous, and shaking would
only cloud it — which is closer to why the two are still described as
siblings than any question of who copied whom.
