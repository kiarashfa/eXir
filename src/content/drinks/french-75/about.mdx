  A gin sour lengthened with sparkling wine, named for a WWI-era field gun and
  documented in conflicting versions from its earliest years, with a
  cognac-based variant that never displaced gin as the drink's usual base.

The name refers to the French 75-millimetre field gun of the First World War,
and the drink is first recorded close to the war itself, though the earliest
sources disagree on its inventor. *The Washington Herald* described a version
in 1915 built from gin, grenadine, applejack and a dash of lemon juice, with
no champagne in it at all <Cite ref="diffords"/>. Robert Vermeire's 1922
cocktail book credited a bartender at Henry's Bar in Paris as the drink's
originator <Cite ref="diffords"/>, while Harry MacElhone's own 1919 guide
credited a different bartender, MacGarry of Buck's Club in London, and
described a build closer to a Tom Collins with champagne standing in for the
soda <Cite ref="morgenthaler"/>. The gin, lemon, sugar and champagne form
familiar today is first set out in Judge Jr.'s 1927 *Here's How*
<Cite ref="diffords"/>.

The drink was served long, over ice in a tall glass, well into the twentieth
century — Harry Craddock's Savoy Cocktail Book of 1930 still calls for it that
way <Cite ref="diffords"/>. The short pour into a champagne flute with no ice,
now the drink's most common serving, is a later habit rather than the
original one.

A cognac-based version has its own advocates: David Embury argued in 1948
that cognac, not gin, was the only base that should carry the name, and
cognac French 75s are still served in some American bars <Cite ref="diffords"/>.
The claim did not carry the wider trade; gin remained, and remains, the
version most bartenders and guides mean by the name.
