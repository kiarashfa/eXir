The first published recipe appears in *Recipes for Mixed Drinks*, a 1916 guide
by Hugo Ensslin, then head bartender at the Hotel Wallick in New York
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. Ensslin's version calls for gin, lemon juice, maraschino
liqueur and crème de violette, in a build close to the one used today
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The recipe's later history is a dispute about a single ingredient. Harry
Craddock's *Savoy Cocktail Book*, published in 1930 and far more widely read
than Ensslin's own guide, prints the Aviation without the violette — gin,
lemon and maraschino only <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Craddock's version is usually
explained as a practical substitution rather than a stylistic one: crème de
violette had become difficult to find by the time he was writing, and a
bartender working from his book had no easy way to follow the earlier recipe
even if they wanted to <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The two versions coexisted in print
for decades, and both are still made under the same name — a gin sour with a
citrus-and-maraschino balance either sits on its own or carries a faint
violet top note, depending on which book a bartender learned it from.

Crème Yvette, a spiced violet liqueur, is sometimes used in place of crème de
violette where the latter is unavailable, which has added a second point of
variation on top of the first <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
