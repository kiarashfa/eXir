Quinine, extracted from cinchona bark, was established as a treatment and
preventive for malaria by the Edinburgh physician George Cleghorn in the
18th century, and British personnel in colonial India took it regularly for
that reason <Cite ref="smithsonian"/>. Commercial quinine tonic water was
patented in 1858 by Erasmus Bond, but a dose concentrated enough to matter
medically was intensely bitter, and mixing it with gin and sugar was a way
of getting it down rather than an accident of taste <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The tidy version of that story — soldiers on ration inventing a cocktail
to fight off malaria — does not hold up. The historian Anistatia Miller has
stated plainly that the gin and tonic was not invented in India as an
antimalarial and that British troops did not drink it as part of their
rations; the romantic imperial framing was largely a 20th-century
retelling, even as colonial India genuinely helped popularise the drink
among travellers returning home in the 1870s <Cite ref="smithsonian"/>.

The gap between the historical dose and the modern one is real and
measurable. A 2004 clinical study found that only very large volumes of
today's tonic water — on the order of half a litre to a litre — could
produce a temporarily therapeutic quinine level, and that sustaining a dose
that way is not a practical defence against the disease; modern tonic is
sweetened and far less bitter than its medicinal ancestor precisely because
it carries a fraction of the quinine <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Whatever the
gin and tonic still owes to its origin as a way of taking medicine, it is
not a protection against malaria today.
