The drink is credited to Joe Sheridan, a chef at the Foynes flying-boat
terminal in County Limerick, sometime around 1943, when a transatlantic
flight turned back in bad weather and a cabin of cold, tired passengers
returned to the terminal for the night <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Sheridan is
said to have warmed coffee with Irish whiskey and sugar and finished it with
cream, and when a passenger asked whether it was Brazilian coffee, to have
answered that it was Irish coffee instead <Cite ref="kqed"/>.

The drink reached the United States by a more documented route. Stanton
Delaplane, a travel writer for the San Francisco Chronicle, drank it at
Shannon and brought the idea to Jack Koeppler at San Francisco's Buena Vista
Cafe, and the two spent time trying to reproduce it before Koeppler travelled
to Foynes to learn the method directly from Sheridan <Cite ref="kqed"/>. The
Buena Vista began serving it on 10 November 1952, using cream aged briefly to
thicken it enough to float, and the recipe is reported to have been
essentially unchanged there since <Cite ref="kqed"/>. San Francisco is
sometimes credited as the drink's birthplace outright; the more accurate
claim, and the one both sources support, is that it was introduced to America
there rather than invented there <Cite ref="kqed"/>.

The recipe was formal enough by 1988 that Ireland's own National Standards
Authority published a specification for it, I.S. 417, since cancelled but
evidence of how codified the proportions had become <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
The float itself is the drink's defining technique: cream poured gently
enough to sit on the surface rather than stirred through, so the coffee stays
hot and dark under a cold, unmixed layer, drunk through rather than after it.
