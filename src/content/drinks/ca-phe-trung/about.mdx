The drink's invention is credited, by family account, to Nguyen Giang, the
founder of what is now Café Giảng in Hanoi's Old Quarter. His son's version
of events, recorded by Wikipedia's editors rather than independently
verified, holds that Giang worked out the recipe in the late 1940s, when
fresh milk was scarce in Vietnam and condensed milk on its own no longer
stretched far enough to make the cappuccinos and milk coffee the era's cafes
served — so he beat egg yolk into it instead, for the same body and richness
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That is a family's account of its own founder, the
same category of claim the brief's own caution about cocktail lore applies
to, and it is presented here as attributed rather than settled.

What is independently verifiable is that Café Giảng itself dates from 1946
and has operated continuously since, treating the recipe as something handed
down through the family rather than published <Cite ref="cafegiang"/>. The
cafe's own account emphasises inheritance and craft over any specific claim
of invention, which is a notably more modest position than the story usually
told about it elsewhere.

However it began, the technique is distinctive enough to have spread well
beyond the one shop credited with it: raw egg yolk is beaten at length with
condensed milk until it turns pale and holds air, then spooned over hot black
coffee brewed through a phin rather than mixed into it, so the two sit as
separate layers — a dessert-like foam over a bitter, smoky robusta base
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Cafes across Hanoi now serve versions of it, most
still built on the same two ingredients and the same long whisk.
