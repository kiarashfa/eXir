The story both sources tell agrees on its shape, if not on every detail. At
the 1957 International Trade Fair in Thessaloniki, Dimitris Vakondios, an
employee of the Nestlé representative exhibiting a shaken chocolate drink for
children, wanted his usual cup of Nescafé on a break and could not find hot
water <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. He mixed the instant coffee with cold water and
ice in the shaker that was on hand for the cocoa demonstration instead, and
what came out — a thick, pale foam over a cold, sweet coffee — is the drink
now served across Greece and Cyprus <Cite ref="vice"/>.

The accident depended on the product as much as the moment. Spray-dried
instant coffee carries almost none of roasted coffee's natural oil, which is
exactly what lets it foam and hold air the way ground coffee cannot
<Cite ref="vice"/>. Vakondios's improvisation only worked because Nescafé, not
brewed coffee, was what he had on hand.

Its spread from a fairground accident to a national habit took decades and a
deliberate push. Nestlé ran sustained advertising for the drink through the
1970s and 1980s, and the campaign's jingle — Greek for "stop for a moment,
make a Nescafé" — is still recalled by Greeks who grew up with it
<Cite ref="vice"/>. The result outgrew the brand that built it: frappé is now
made with any instant coffee, shaken or blended into foam, poured over ice
and cold water, and treated as a fixture of outdoor café culture rather than
a product endorsement <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.
