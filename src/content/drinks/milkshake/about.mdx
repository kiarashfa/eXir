"Milkshake" first appeared in print in 1885, naming a drink that had nothing
in common with the one served under that name today: whiskey, eggs and cream,
served as a tonic rather than a treat <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="hankering"/>.
By around 1900 the name had shifted to a sweeter, non-alcoholic drink flavoured
with chocolate, strawberry or vanilla syrup, and customers were already asking
soda fountains to add ice cream to it <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

A second ingredient arrived in between: malted milk powder, a mix of malted
barley, wheat flour and dried milk that William Horlick had patented in 1897 as
a health tonic rather than a drink <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Soda fountains and
pharmacies took up "malted milk" as a standard order well before ice cream
entered it.

The version recognisable today dates to a specific counter: in 1922, Ivar
"Pop" Coulson, working the soda fountain at a Walgreens in Chicago, added two
scoops of vanilla ice cream, made in the chain's own plant, to the standard
malted milk drink <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="hankering"/>. The combination
sold under the name Horlick's Malted Milk, and the thickness the ice cream
gave it is what separated the drink from the thinner malted it was built on
<Cite ref="hankering"/>. Automating the result took longer: Earl Prince's Multimixer, introduced in
1936, could turn out five milkshakes from one motor at once, and mechanised a
drink that had until then been mixed and shaken by hand at the counter
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.
