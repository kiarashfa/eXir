The earliest documented reference to a Cobbler is an 1838 diary entry by
Katherine Jane Ellice, a Canadian traveller in the United States, identified
by drinks historian David Wondrich as the drink's first known written
mention <Cite ref="difford"/>. The style was already common in American
taverns by the end of that decade <Cite ref="wiki"/>, and it reached formal
print in Jerry Thomas's 1862 *The Bar-Tender's Guide*, the first book
written specifically for bartenders, in a three-ingredient form of sherry,
sugar and orange <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The Cobbler's rise is tied directly to the drinking straw's own history.
Wondrich describes it as the straw's "killer app": crushed ice packed
against bare lips is uncomfortable to drink through, and the Cobbler is
credited with bringing the straw into common American use before paper
straws were even patented, when rye straw and hollow pasta served the same
purpose <Cite ref="difford"/>. Charles Dickens gave the drink wider notice
by featuring it in *The Life and Adventures of Martin Chuzzlewit*
<Cite ref="wiki"/>, and bartender Harry Johnson called it, in his 1888
manual, the most popular beverage in the country with ladies and gentlemen
alike <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The Cobbler's popularity did not survive Prohibition. Cocktail authority
David Embury, writing in 1949, noted that Cobblers as a category never
really re-emerged once legal bars reopened <Cite ref="difford"/>, and the
style has since been a period revival rather than a drink that stayed in
continuous circulation.
