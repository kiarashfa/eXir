Steeping coffee in cold water rather than hot is old enough to predate
espresso, filter brewing and most of the equipment coffee is now made with.
One account traces the practice to Dutch traders in the seventeenth century,
who are said to have brewed a cold concentrate for the practical reason that
it needed no open flame and travelled well by ship, and to have carried the
method to Japan, where it settled alongside an existing culture of cold-
steeped tea <Cite ref="dailycoffeenews"/>. That lineage is offered as a
likely account rather than a documented one, and no source here confirms it
with a primary record.

The method's modern, commercial form is much more precisely dated. Todd
Simpson, trained as a chemist, is credited with developing what became the
Toddy cold-brew system after encountering the technique on his travels;
accounts differ on exactly where, but agree he built the first version in
his own garage and originally sold it as a low-acid concentrate for people
whose stomachs reacted badly to hot-brewed coffee <Cite ref="sagebrush"/>.
That framing — cold brew as a digestive accommodation rather than a flavour
choice — was the product's original pitch, and it took decades more before
the specialty coffee industry adopted the same chemistry for its own reasons.

What cold water changes is extraction rather than strength: without heat to
pull them out quickly, the acids and some of the more volatile aromatics
that give hot coffee its brightness come out slowly and incompletely even
over many hours, which is the entire reason the method needs hours rather
than minutes and produces a rounder, less acidic cup than the same beans
brewed hot and then chilled <Cite ref="dailycoffeenews"/>.
