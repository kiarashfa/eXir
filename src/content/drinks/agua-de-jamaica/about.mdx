The plant behind agua de jamaica, *Hibiscus sabdariffa*, was domesticated in
West Africa, in the region of the Western Sudan <Cite ref="wiki-hibiscus"/>,
and reached Mexico only after that — the flower sold in every market there
today arrived from elsewhere. How it crossed the Atlantic is not settled.
One account has it carried by enslaved Africans and first cultivated in
Brazil <Cite ref="wiki-hibiscus"/>; another traces it across the Pacific
instead, aboard the Manila galleons that ran from the Philippines to Acapulco
from 1565 onward, a route that would have brought the plant to Mexico
directly rather than by way of South America <Cite ref="lolomercadito"/>.
Both routes agree on the same starting point in tropical Africa and simply
disagree about which ocean the flower crossed to get to New Spain.

Once established, the calyx became a fixture of the Mexican agua fresca
tradition: a large dried harvest, sold loose or bagged, steeped hot and
strained into a tart red concentrate that is sweetened and diluted to taste.
The same plant produces a family of near-identical drinks under different
names wherever it took root — bissap in Senegal, sorrel in the Caribbean,
karkadeh in Egypt and the Middle East <Cite ref="196flavors"/> — and Mexico's
version is one branch of that spread rather than an independent invention.
What distinguishes it from its West African relative is less the flower than
the glass it ends up in: a lighter hand with the sugar, and a place next to
horchata and tamarindo on the same drinks-stand list of aguas frescas, rather
than the heavier sweetening and added spice more typical of its West African
counterpart.
