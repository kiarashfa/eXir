The drink is credited to Ada Coleman, who ran the American Bar at the Savoy
Hotel in London from 1903 <Cite ref="punch"/>. In her own account, given in a
1925 newspaper interview, the actor Charles Hawtrey would come in tired and
ask for "something with a bit of punch in it"; she worked on the recipe until
she had one, and Hawtrey's verdict on tasting it — "that is the real
hanky-panky" — gave it its name <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Harry Craddock
printed the recipe in *The Savoy Cocktail Book* in 1930, some years after
Coleman herself had left the bar <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The drink is built as a sweet martini — equal parts gin and sweet vermouth -
carried by a dash of Fernet-Branca, and it is sometimes credited as the
earliest printed cocktail recipe to use Fernet as a modifier, at a time when
the amaro was thought of as a medicine-cabinet bottle rather than a bar one
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That reputation rests on print survival rather than
on any claim to have been first to pour it that way, and it is worth stating
with the same hedge the record itself carries.

The gin-and-sweet-vermouth base puts the drink in the same general shape as
the Martinez and the early Martini, without descending from either by name:
"sweet martini" was a generic description for a whole style of drink in this
period, not a specific recipe Coleman is known to have been adapting
<Cite ref="diffords"/>.
