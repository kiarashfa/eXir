The word appears in American print by around 1770, in Robert Munford's satire
*The Candidate*, which names a character Mr. Julip, and the drink itself is
described a few decades later: an English visitor's 1803 account of Virginia
records a morning dram of spirits with mint steeped in it, and a Virginia
clergyman had already described "a tumbler of rum and water, well sweetened,
with a slip of mint in it" in 1793 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Bourbon is not original to the drink. Rum and brandy were the early bases, and
gin-based versions made with genever were common through the nineteenth
century; a bourbon julep only became the dominant form in the twentieth
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Crushed ice is nearly as old as the drink in America —
Virginia taverns had ice houses by the 1780s, and an "iced julep" is
advertised in a Norfolk newspaper as early as 1807 — which makes the julep one
of the first American drinks built around ice rather than served merely
cooled <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The convention of serving it in a silver or
pewter cup, held only by its rim and foot so that frost forms untouched on the
sides, is part of the same early tradition <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

Its association with horse racing predates the Kentucky Derby itself: silver
julep cups were already being awarded as race trophies by the 1820s
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The Derby began in 1875, but the julep's status as
its official drink is a later, and heavily promoted, development — Churchill
Downs has marketed it that way only since 1938 <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>, and
the track's own account now describes it as tied to the race for "nearly a
century" rather than since the race's founding <Cite ref="derby"/>. The scale
is now considerable: Churchill Downs reports well over a hundred thousand
juleps served across Kentucky Oaks and Derby weekend, drawing on thousands of
bottles of bourbon <Cite ref="derby"/>.
