The tea itself came to Morocco by accident of war. Gunpowder green tea —
tightly rolled Chinese leaf named for its resemblance to shot — was bound
for Baltic markets when the Crimean War closed those ports to British
shipping in the 1850s, and merchants carrying the surplus stock sailed it
south instead, selling it cheaply through the Moroccan ports of Tangier and
Essaouira <Cite ref="teatrade"/> <Cite ref="corridor"/>. Moroccans were
already growing spearmint for medicinal use, and combining the two into one
drink took hold quickly: within a generation it had become the country's
defining domestic beverage <Cite ref="corridor"/>.

The pour is as much a part of the drink as the leaf. Tea is lifted well
above the glass before it is poured, which cools it slightly and aerates it
enough to raise a thin foam on top, known locally as the "turban" — regarded
as proof the tea has been made properly rather than as decoration
<Cite ref="teatrade"/> <Cite ref="corridor"/>. The same leaves are then
used for three successive rounds, each weaker and differently balanced than
the last, matched in a well-known saying to three stages of life: the
first bitter, the second sweet, the third gentle <Cite ref="corridor"/>.

Serving it is treated as an act of welcome rather than refreshment alone.
A Tuareg proverb given in one account holds that tea needs three things —
time, embers and friends — and preparing it in front of a guest, rather
than handing over a finished glass, is understood as the more meaningful
gesture <Cite ref="corridor"/>.
