Two Taiwanese tea houses claim the drink, and neither claim has ever been
settled. Hanlin Tea Room in Tainan says its founder, Tu Tsung-ho, saw white
tapioca balls for sale at a local market in 1986 and brought them home to add
to milk tea <Cite ref="taipeitimes"/>. Chun Shui Tang in Taichung says one of
its staff, a twenty-year-old employee named Lin Hsiu-hui, mixed tapioca balls
— her own favourite childhood snack — into iced milk tea the following year,
in 1987 <Cite ref="natgeo"/>. Both versions agree on the shape of the story,
a teahouse experimenting with a familiar dessert dropped into a familiar
drink; they disagree only on which teahouse and which year got there first.

The dispute outlasted the drink's own rise to ubiquity. The two chains sued
each other over the claim, and the case dragged on for years before a court
ruled in 2019 that because bubble tea was never patented, who actually
invented it was legally irrelevant <Cite ref="natgeo"/>. By the mid-1990s the
drink was already standard on tea house menus across Taiwan regardless of
which shop reached it first <Cite ref="taipeitimes"/>.

The pearls themselves are a separate craft from the tea. They are rolled
from tapioca starch, drawn from the cassava root, and are traditionally
darkened and sweetened with brown sugar rather than left plain
<Cite ref="natgeo"/> — this page uses a clear demerara syrup instead, a close
but not identical substitute. Early versions of the drink used black tea and
lactose-free creamer rather than fresh milk, and the fruit-forward version
served alongside it here is a later variation on the same base: the tea and
the pearls stay constant, and what changes is only what sweetens and colours
the glass around them.
