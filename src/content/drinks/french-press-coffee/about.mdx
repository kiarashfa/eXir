The earliest patent for a plunger pot belongs to two French inventors, Henri-Otto
Mayer and Jacques-Victor Delforge, in 1852, but their design left no seal
around the filter and pushed as much sediment up as it held back
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The mechanism that fixed that problem, a spring-loaded
disc sealing against the glass as it descends, was patented by the Milanese
designers Giulio Moneta and Attilio Calimani in 1929, and it is their design
that the modern press descends from rather than the earlier French one
<Cite ref="pdg"/>.

The most widely sold version came later still. Faliero Bondanini patented a
further refinement in 1958 and had it manufactured in France, at a factory
that had previously made clarinets, under the name Chambord
<Cite ref="pdg"/>. That pot, sold in Britain as La Cafetière and eventually
produced by the Danish company Bodum, is the shape now most associated with
the device, despite the method's Italian mechanism and the earlier French
patent it is popularly named for <Cite ref="pdg"/>.

The brewing principle has stayed constant while the hardware changed hands:
coarse grounds steep loose in the full volume of water, with nothing filtering
them until the plunger is pressed, so the cup carries the coffee's oils and
fine sediment in a way a paper filter strains out <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That
is also the method's one real fault. Coffee left standing on its grounds after
the plunge continues to extract through the wet bed trapped beneath the mesh,
which is why a press left on the table rather than decanted keeps getting more
bitter the longer it sits.
