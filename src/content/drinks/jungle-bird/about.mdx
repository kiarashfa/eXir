The drink is credited to Jeffrey Ong, beverage manager at the Aviary Bar in
the Kuala Lumpur Hilton, who created it as a welcome drink for arriving
guests and named it for the caged tropical birds visible from the bar
<Cite ref="wiki"/>. Sources disagree on the year: some place it in 1973 and
others in 1978 <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="wonk"/>.

It stayed obscure for over a decade. The first print appearance was in John
J. Poister's *The New American Bartender's Guide* in 1989, which called for a
generic dark rum <Cite ref="wiki"/>. American tiki historian Jeff "Beachbum"
Berry found it there and reprinted it in his own 2002 book *Intoxica!*,
specifying Jamaican dark rum in place of the generic bottle and noting that
what made the drink worth including was the Campari — an amaro in a tropical
drink was an unusual combination for its period <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The recipe now treated as standard dates to 2010, when bartender Giuseppe
González at the New York bar Painkiller switched the rum to a blackstrap
style and cut back the pineapple juice; that version is the one most
bartenders now reach for <Cite ref="wiki"/>. The drink's wider popularity
followed over the next several years as part of a broader revival of tiki
drinks built on real technique rather than novelty, and it was recognised as
an official cocktail by the International Bartenders Association in 2024
<Cite ref="wiki"/>.
