The powder comes from the tubers of wild orchids, ground into a flour whose
starch, glucomannan, is what gives it its thickening power — a substance
strong enough that only a small spoonful sets a whole pot of milk
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Its use as a drink long predates the Ottoman period
it is most associated with: Roman and Greek writers described similar
orchid-root preparations, called satyrion and priapiscus, which they
credited with restorative and aphrodisiac powers rather than valuing them
as a simple hot drink <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The word itself is generally traced to Turkish, and salep held a settled
place in Ottoman food culture, including a folk reputation as something fed
to young women before marriage to help them gain weight
<Cite ref="millerstreet"/>. From there it travelled further than most hot
drinks of its era did: salep reached England and Germany by the seventeenth
century, sold in London as "saloop" from street stalls to working
Londoners as a cheaper alternative before coffee and tea became the
everyday choice <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

The orchid tubers the drink depends on are slow to harvest and now
increasingly restricted for conservation reasons, which is the practical
reason this page and the Levantine sahlab page next to it have diverged in
practice as much as in name: where the tuber remains available, as it does
in parts of Turkey, the drink is still made from real salep flour, ground
cinnamon dusted over the top rather than any fruit flavouring
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. Sahlab, covered separately on this site, keeps the
name and the texture but substitutes a starch that does not carry the same
supply pressure.
