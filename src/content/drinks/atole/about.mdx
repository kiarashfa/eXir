The name comes directly from Nahuatl: *ātōlli*, meaning roughly "watered
down" or "slurry," a description of the drink's texture rather than a brand
or a place <Cite ref="mysliceofmexico"/>. It is old enough to appear in the
painted inscriptions on Classic Maya vessels, where atole is named alongside
cacao among the primary ingredients recorded — evidence that the drink held
enough ceremonial and everyday weight in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica to be
worth writing on pottery <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>.

What made atole possible at all was nixtamalization, the pre-Hispanic
technique of treating dried corn with an alkaline solution before grinding
it. The process breaks down the kernel's hardened outer shell and makes its
niacin available to the body, and without it a corn-dependent diet risks
pellagra; both sources treat the corn used for atole as this treated masa
rather than raw ground grain <Cite ref="mysliceofmexico"/>. The earliest
version was masa dissolved directly in water and sweetened with honey or
agave syrup, sometimes flavoured with fruit, chile or cacao
<Cite ref="mysliceofmexico"/>.

The drink changed rather than disappeared after the Spanish conquest. Milk,
refined and unrefined cane sugar, cinnamon and vanilla were folded into
recipes that had previously run on water, honey and native spices, and the
modern standard — masa thickened in milk with piloncillo, cinnamon and
vanilla — is a colonial-era hybrid built on a pre-Hispanic base rather than
either tradition on its own <Cite ref="mysliceofmexico"/>. Atole's place at
Day of the Dead altars and at Las Posadas gatherings continues a use the
drink is recorded as having long before either Spanish holiday existed to
attach it to.
