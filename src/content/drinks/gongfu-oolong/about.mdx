The name describes a method rather than a drink: *gongfu* means skill or
effort applied with care, and gongfu tea is the technique of brewing one dose
of leaf through several short infusions rather than one long steep
<Cite ref="wiki-gongfu"/>. Its home region is contested between two areas —
the Wuyi Mountains of Fujian, where the oolong trade for export first
developed, and the Chaoshan area of eastern Guangdong, whose largest city,
Chaozhou, is now treated as the practice's capital <Cite ref="wiki-gongfu"/>
<Cite ref="pathofcha"/>. Sources that favour Chaozhou point to a lineage
running back to at least the eighteenth century, though the exact starting
point is not agreed on, and documents from the 1940s were still describing
the technique specifically as "Chaoshan gongfu tea" rather than treating it as
a general Chinese practice <Cite ref="wiki-gongfu"/>.

For most of that history it stayed a regional habit rather than a national
one. It was Taiwanese tea practitioners in the 1970s who reworked it into a
more formal, more widely taught style, and that revised version is what
carried gongfu brewing back into mainland China and out into the rest of the
world, well beyond where it started <Cite ref="pathofcha"/>.

The technique itself has not changed to fit that spread. A small vessel — a
gaiwan or a tiny pot — takes a dose of leaf disproportionately large for its
size, is rinsed with a quick discarded pour to wake the leaf, and is then
refilled with fresh water for a rapid succession of infusions, the first
barely longer than the rinse and each one after it a little longer than the
last <Cite ref="wiki-gongfu"/>. The strength that produces is not the
strength of a long steep: it is short contact repeated, which is a
categorically different way of extracting the same leaf.
