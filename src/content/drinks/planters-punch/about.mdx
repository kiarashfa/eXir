The drink's proportion is older than any surviving recipe for it. One part
sour, two of sweet, three of strong and four of weak was printed as a verse
in the London magazine *Fun* in September 1878, itself framed as a recipe
already known in Jamaica rather than a new invention <Cite ref="wiki"/>. Punch
made to that shape — spirit, citrus, sugar and water in fixed ratio — is
older still and reached the Caribbean from India, carried by English traders
who mixed arrack, lime, sugar, spice and water there long before rum entered
the formula <Cite ref="vinepair"/>.

Planter's Punch itself became closely associated with Jamaica through the
early twentieth century, served at hotels including the island's Myrtle Bank
as punches gave way to a single measured serving rather than a shared bowl
<Cite ref="vinepair"/>. Its spread beyond the Caribbean owes largely to Fred
L. Myers, whose family rum company sent a bartender to mix Planter's Punches
at the West Indies Pavilion of the British Empire Exhibition in London in
1924, putting the drink and the Myers's name in front of an international
audience at once <Cite ref="vinepair"/>.

The rhyme survives better than any single fixed recipe, and the drink is
better understood as a proportion carried forward through two centuries than
as a formula anyone can date precisely.
