Karkade is made from the dried calyces of *Hibiscus sabdariffa*, a plant
grown across Egypt, Sudan and Ethiopia and used here for the drink rather
than for the fibre or edible seed it is also cultivated for elsewhere
<Cite ref="egyptianstreets"/>. The petals are boiled rather than simply
steeped, then strained and sweetened, and the same brew is served hot in
winter or poured over ice in summer <Cite ref="wiki"/>.

The drink is often called "the tea of the pharaohs," a claim traced back to
a supposed appearance in the Ebers Papyrus of around 1550 BC. Both sources
treat the label as folklore rather than settled history: the papyrus
reference is disputed, and the phrase itself appears to have been coined by
popular writers rather than scholars of the period <Cite ref="wiki"/>
<Cite ref="egyptianstreets"/>.

What is better documented is karkade's present-day place in Egyptian and
Sudanese life. It is poured at weddings as a toast associated with joy and
prosperity, and it is a fixture of Ramadan, served at iftar to break the
day's fast <Cite ref="wiki"/> <Cite ref="egyptianstreets"/>. In Muslim
communities it also does the job a toast of wine or spirits would do
elsewhere, standing in as the drink of celebration at gatherings where
alcohol has no place <Cite ref="egyptianstreets"/>. Preparation has
stayed a household task rather than a commercial one: the petals are
still most often boiled at home, with tea bags treated as a weaker,
convenience substitute for the real thing <Cite ref="wiki"/>.
