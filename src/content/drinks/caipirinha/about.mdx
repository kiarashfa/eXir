The name is a diminutive of *caipira*, the word for someone from Brazil's
rural interior, roughly the Brazilian equivalent of "hillbilly" used
affectionately rather than as an insult <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. What that
name is actually attached to — a specific date and inventor for the drink —
is where the accounts disagree.

One version, drawing on the reporting of cocktail historian David Wondrich,
places the caipirinha's emergence in the mid-nineteenth-century countryside
near São Paulo's coastal rainforest, made by rural workers from cachaça,
lime and sugar as a cheap, fortifying daily ration rather than a cocktail in
the modern sense <Cite ref="punch"/>. On this account it is "all but
impossible to pin down to a single originator," and the earliest solid
documentation under the name Batida Paulista does not appear until travel
writing in the 1940s, with the drink not reaching international attention
until the late 1970s and early 1980s <Cite ref="punch"/>.

A separate, more specific account places the invention in 1918, in the
interior town of Piracicaba, credited to landowning farmers who are said to
have developed it from an older Portuguese cold remedy of lemon, garlic and
honey, gradually replacing the medicinal ingredients with sugar and ice
<Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. The two stories do not describe the same event, and
neither is treated here as settled; what both agree on is that the drink is
regional in origin, tied to cachaça specifically rather than rum, and far
older as an everyday preparation than as an internationally served cocktail.

The caipirinha received Brazilian Cultural Heritage status in 2003 and is
recognised as an official cocktail by the International Bartenders
Association <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>, formal status that arrived long after
whichever of its origin stories is true.
