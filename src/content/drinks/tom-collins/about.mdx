The drink descends from the John Collins, a gin punch attested by 1869 and
attributed to a headwaiter of that name at Limmer's Old House, a London
hotel and coffee house active in the early nineteenth century
<Cite ref="wiki-john"/>. That 1869 recipe specifically called for Old Tom
gin, a slightly sweetened style distinct from the London dry gin common
today, and it is this ingredient — not a different inventor — that drink
historian David Wondrich points to as the likely source of the new name
<Cite ref="wiki-john"/>.

The name's spread was helped along by an unrelated event: in 1874, a
citywide hoax swept New York and then other American cities, in which one
person would goad a friend into angrily demanding "Tom Collins" from a
bartender, who was in on the joke and would point the victim toward a
stranger <Cite ref="distilledhistory"/>. Newspapers ran along with it,
printing fabricated sightings and even songs about the fictional Tom
Collins, and the name became a piece of shared slang before it was
attached to any drink <Cite ref="distilledhistory"/>. Jerry Thomas printed
the first known recipe under the name "Tom Collins" in the 1876 edition of
his bartending guide, by which point the joke and the Old Tom gin recipe
had merged into one name <Cite ref="distilledhistory"/> <Cite ref="wiki-john"/>.

The naming has since drifted again: "Tom Collins" is now used for the gin
version specifically, while "John Collins" has come to mean the same build
made with a different base spirit, most often whiskey — close to the
reverse of how the two names first split apart <Cite ref="wiki-john"/>.
