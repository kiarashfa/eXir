The word tepache comes from the Nahuatl *tepiātl*, recorded as a drink made
from raw corn and given to those feeling faint, and the drink's roots
among the Nahua people of central Mexico go back to before the Spanish
conquest <Cite ref="wikipedia"/>. That corn-based drink is a different thing
from the pineapple ferment sold under the same name today, and the written
record shows tepache was never one fixed recipe even once it moved away
from corn: a Spanish colonial report from 1791 already lists more than one
version, including one made by fermenting pulque with brown sugar and
anise, and an 1891 bulletin from Puebla instead describes fermenting brown
sugar in a maceration of pineapple rind with spices, much closer to the
drink most people now mean by the name <Cite ref="drinkingfolk"/>.

Pineapple's dominance is therefore a real but gradual shift rather than a
single dated event, and even now it is not universal: vendors across
Mexico also make tepache from tamarind, apple, banana and other fruit,
fermented the same way <Cite ref="drinkingfolk"/>. What has stayed constant
is the method rather than the fruit — a short, spontaneous fermentation
driven by wild yeast, typically finished within a couple of days, that
produces a tangy, faintly fizzy drink with only a trace of alcohol rather
than a genuinely intoxicating one <Cite ref="wikipedia"/><Cite ref="drinkingfolk"/>.
