name: The Old Fashioned
summary: >-
  Spirit, sugar, bitters and water. The oldest shape in the book and the one
  every other stirred drink is a rearrangement of.
---

The formula is a definition rather than a recipe: a spirit, something sweet,
something bitter and some water. It is what the word cocktail meant before the
word meant anything else, and the drink now called an Old Fashioned is named for
people asking for it the old-fashioned way once the word had drifted.

Four slots, and each one does a job the others cannot.

The **spirit** carries everything. Nothing else in the drink has enough presence
to hide behind, which is why this family is where a bottle's character shows
most plainly and why swapping the spirit produces a different drink rather than
a variation.

The **sweetener** is not there to make the drink sweet. It is there to give the
alcohol something to sit on: a spirit-forward drink without it reads thin and
hot rather than dry. Moving it changes the texture before it changes the taste.

The **bitter** element is the one that turns a strong drink into a composed one.
In the original it is a dash of aromatic bitters. In the drinks descended from
it the slot has grown: a vermouth, a bitter aperitivo, an amaro. That growth is
the family's whole history — the bitter slot expanding until it is a full third
of the drink is how an Old Fashioned becomes a Manhattan and then a Negroni.

The **water** is the slot nobody writes down. It arrives from the ice during
stirring, it is a quarter to two-fifths of the finished volume, and it is the
difference between a drink and a measure of spirit. Every drink in this family
is a spirit that has been deliberately watered, and treating that as an
ingredient rather than an accident is most of what separates a good one from a
harsh one.

The members diverge on which slot they enlarge. The Manhattan turns the bitter
slot into fortified wine and lets it carry the sweetness too, which is why it
needs no separate sugar. The Negroni takes the equal-parts route and gives the
spirit, the bitter and the sweet the same volume, which is why it comes out
weaker than its reputation and more bitter than its strength suggests. The
Sazerac keeps the original shape and spends its variation on the glass.
