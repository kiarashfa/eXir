Two parts spirit, three quarters of a part citrus, three quarters of a part
sugar. Almost every shaken drink is this shape with one slot changed, and the
proportion is close enough to universal that a bartender given an unfamiliar
spirit will reach for it before anything else.

The ratio exists because sugar and acid cancel each other in a specific way. A
sour is not sweet and it is not sour; it is both at once, and the sensation of
balance comes from the two being present in roughly matching quantities rather
than from either being restrained. Take the sugar out and the acid is
unbearable. Take the acid out and the sugar is cloying. This is why the family
resists the instruction to adjust to taste more than any other: moving one slot
without the other does not make the drink sweeter or sharper, it makes it
unbalanced.

The **spirit** slot is the most open in the book. Whisky, rum, gin, brandy,
pisco, agave and tequila all work, and each named result is its own drink rather
than a variant.

The **citrus** slot is lemon or lime, and the choice is not interchangeable.
Both juices measure near six per cent acid, so the drink's sourness barely
moves; what moves is the aromatic. Lemon reads clean and slightly floral against
brown spirits, lime reads greener and sharper against white ones, and swapping
them is a flavour decision that the arithmetic will not show.

The **sugar** slot is where the family branches. Plain syrup keeps the spirit in
front. A richer syrup delivers the same sweetness in less liquid and leaves the
drink heavier. Honey, orgeat, demerara and maple each bring a flavour of their
own, and several of the drinks with their own names are a sour with nothing
changed but this.

A **fourth slot is optional and defines a sub-branch**: egg white. It adds no
flavour worth mentioning and changes everything about how the drink reads,
turning acid that had been sharp into something rounded, and adding a head that
holds the aromatics over the glass. It also lengthens the shake, which is why
this branch takes on more water than a plain sour and finishes a little weaker.
