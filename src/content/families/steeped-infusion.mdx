name: Steeped Infusions
summary: >-
  Leaf, flower or root left in hot water for a measured time, then taken out.
  The one family with no ratio at its centre — the variables are temperature and
  time.
---

Every other family in this book is a proportion. This one is a process, and the
proportion is the least interesting thing about it. Two grams of leaf in two
hundred millilitres of water will make a thin tea, a good tea or an undrinkable
one depending on nothing but how hot the water was and how long the leaf stayed
in it, and no amount of adjusting the ratio recovers a brew that got either of
those wrong.

There are four variables and only one of them is a quantity.

The **dose** is how much solid goes in, and it is the slot with the widest
legitimate range in the book. A mug of builder's tea is about three grams to a
quarter of a litre. A gongfu session is five grams to a hundred millilitres —
more than four times the strength — and it is not a stronger drink, because the
leaf is in contact with the water for seconds rather than minutes. Dose and time
trade against each other, which is why a brew ratio quoted without a time means
nothing.

The **temperature** decides what comes out, not just how fast. Extraction is not
one process but several running at different rates: aromatics leave first,
then the caffeine and the sugars, and last the larger tannins that read as
astringency. Water below boiling never reaches the tannins in quantity, which is
why a green tea brewed at eighty degrees is sweet and the same leaf at boiling
is harsh. Water at a full boil is right for a robust black leaf, which needs the
heat to open at all, and wrong for almost everything delicate.

The **time** is the other half of the same equation and the one that is actually
controllable in a domestic kitchen. It is also the only variable in this book
that the site deliberately refuses to scale: brewing ten cups does not take ten
times as long, because the leaf and the water scale together and the extraction
does not care how big the pot is.

The **removal** is a step, not an afterthought. An infusion left on its leaf is
not finished — it is still brewing, more slowly, and by the second cup it is a
different drink. This is the structural difference between an infusion and a
decoction, and it is why the family's members are described by when the solid
comes out rather than by what went in.

A branch of the family never separates the two at all. The chai and karak
traditions boil the leaf with milk and spice rather than steeping it, and the
milk changes the chemistry as well as the taste: its proteins bind the same
tannins that would otherwise read as astringency, which is why a tea strong
enough to be unpleasant black is exactly right with milk in it. Those members
are boiled rather than steeped, they are timed from the boil, and they take
doses that would be absurd in a teapot.

What holds all of this together is not a formula but an order of operations:
heat the water to a stated temperature, wet a stated weight of solid, wait a
stated time, and separate them. Where a member states a ratio, the ratio belongs
to that member and to its own time and temperature. It does not transfer, and
this page will not pretend otherwise by averaging them.
