Pour a spirit into a tall glass, fill it with ice, top it with something cold
and carbonated, and stir once. There is no technique here worth the name, which
is exactly why the family is worth a page: everything that can go wrong goes
wrong in the handling rather than in the recipe.

The **spirit** is a single part and a modest pour. A highball is not a weakened
version of a stronger drink — it is a different intention, in which a spirit is
stretched to something you can drink for an hour. That stretching is unforgiving
of subtlety. A bottle whose interest lies in a slow finish loses that finish
under carbonation and cold, so the family belongs to spirits with an immediate,
declarative character: a botanical gin, a smoky Scotch, a bitter amaro, a rum
with molasses still audible in it.

The **mixer** is three parts and does most of the work. It is where the sugar
is, where the acid is when there is any, and where the bubbles are. Carbonation
is not decoration: it carries aroma up out of the glass, and it makes a sweet
mixer taste less sweet than the same liquid flat. This is why a highball made
with a mixer that has gone soft is not a slightly duller drink but a
noticeably sweeter one.

The **ice** is the third slot and the one people cut. Ice does two jobs at once
and they pull against each other — it chills, which costs dilution, and it
insulates what is already chilled, which prevents it. A glass packed to the rim
has less liquid surface exposed and a lower net melt rate than a glass with
three cubes rattling in it, so the correct answer to "how much ice" is always
"more". Cold mixer helps for the same reason: every degree the mixer is above
freezing is paid for in melted ice.

The order matters more than in any other family. Spirit first, ice next, mixer
last, and one gentle lift with a bar spoon rather than a stir — carbonation
knocked out of the drink during building does not come back, and a highball
stirred like an Old Fashioned arrives flat.

The family's members diverge on what the mixer is rather than on what the ratio
is. Soda water adds nothing but length and bubbles, and leaves the spirit to
carry the drink alone. Tonic adds quinine's bitterness and a real quantity of
sugar, which is why it flatters gin's botanicals and overwhelms quieter
spirits. Ginger beer brings its own heat. Cola brings sweetness, acid and
caramel at once, and is doing more of the work than its reputation suggests. A
handful of members replace the base spirit with two bitter aperitifs in equal
measure and top that instead, which is where this family and the Old Fashioned's
bitter-slot branch meet.
